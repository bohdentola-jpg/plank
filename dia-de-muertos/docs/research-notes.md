# Research notes — how the research shaped the game

The full research is in two files:

- [research-facts.md](research-facts.md) — dates, origins, every ofrenda element, foods, symbols, regional and world traditions, pronunciation, with sources and confidence tags.
- [research-sensitivity.md](research-sensitivity.md) — what Mexican and Mexican-American educators, museums and writers say about teaching Día de Muertos respectfully, with a design checklist.

Every learning card in the game lists its sources (`src/content/sources.js`), and the in-game **Book of Memories → Sources** page shows them all.

## Rules the game follows

**Framing**
- Día de Muertos is shown as love, memory and family — sad and happy at the same time. No Halloween imagery, no horror cues, no "spooky" palette.
- Beliefs are described, not prescribed: "many families believe…", "in our town…", "some people say…".
- Indigenous roots are credited by naming living peoples (Purépecha, Maya, Nahua, Zapotec, Mixtec…). The game says historians still debate how much of the holiday is Indigenous and how much is Catholic/European — it never claims a "3,000-year-old Aztec festival".
- The fictional town (Santa María del Lago) is rooted in one region — the lake area of Michoacán — and characters point out that other places and families do it differently, and that some families don't celebrate.
- Modern additions are labeled as modern: La Catrina (1910s / 1947), alebrijes (1936), the Mexico City parade (2016), Catrina face paint, pet ofrendas (~2019), *Coco*'s inventions.
- Popular myths are corrected in a dedicated "Myths & facts" chapter (papel picado "color codes", the "required" 7 levels, "La muerte es democrática" as a verified Posada quote, alebrijes as ancient spirit guides…).

**The ofrenda**
- Abuelo Beto is the built-in honoree, so no player has to use a real loss.
- The ofrenda is **never graded**. Any number of levels (1, 2, 3, 7) is accepted, items can go anywhere, and Abuela's "wishes" are story goals, not rules. The "four elements" strip is labeled as one popular explanation.
- Religious items (the cross) are optional; the player is never asked to pray.
- Free mode is optional and offers a relative, a pet, a historical figure, someone you admire or a story character. Players **draw** a portrait — there are no photo uploads — and a gentle note says it's OK to feel sad, happy, both, or nothing in particular.

**Representation and art**
- No sombrero/serape/big-mustache shorthand, no sleeping figures, no tequila jokes, no yellow "Mexico filter". Characters wear everyday clothes (Abuela's rebozo and embroidered blouse are everyday for many grandmothers).
- A range of skin tones with no colorism (the vendors are not the darkest characters); Lupita's friend Mateo is Afro-Mexican and Maestra Eréndira is Purépecha — both modern people.
- The Catrina and Catrín are elegant and friendly, in Posada's satirical spirit — never scary, never sexualized.
- Embroidery and textile patterns are generic, not copied from a specific community.

**Activities**
- Papel picado is made the Huixcolotla way (chisel through a stack), and the town of San Salvador Huixcolotla is named and credited.
- Sugar skulls use alfeñique; Toluca's Feria del Alfeñique is named. Decorating is free-form and unscored, and you can write Abuelo's name on the forehead.
- Pan de muerto: any shape is fine; the baker shares "some say…" meanings and regional variants.
- Calaveritas tease *quirks* of living people with affection — never appearance, identity or grief, and never the deceased grandfather. Free text goes through a kindness filter.
- The cemetery is warm and social. The player only tends their own family's grave; a neighbor models asking before taking photos; praying families can't be interrupted.
- The souls' visit is only suggested through gentle signs from the tradition: petals, candle flames leaning in a warm breeze, a monarch landing on the photo, Chispa wagging at Abuelo's empty chair.
- Music is original and gentle (guitar, marimba). No "Mexican Hat Dance", no mariachi stings.
- Abuelo's favorites are kid-safe (café de olla, hot chocolate, corundas); the book mentions honestly that some families set out grown-up drinks, without making them items.

## Known limits

- The research was done through web searches; some sites could not be opened directly. Items in the "Around the world" chapter come partly from general knowledge and are marked ⚑ on the Sources page.
- The research recommends what a real studio would do next: review by paid Mexican and Mexican-American consultants (including Purépecha and Afro-Mexican voices), a Mexican educator and a child-grief specialist, and playtesting with Mexican and Mexican-American families. For a class project, showing the game to people from the community and listening to their feedback is the best next step.
