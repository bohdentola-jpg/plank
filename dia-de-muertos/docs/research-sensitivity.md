# Día de Muertos: Cultural-Sensitivity Research for an Educational Kids' Game

*Point-and-click game for ages ~8–13. Fictional Mexican town. Characters: a girl, her Xoloitzcuintle, her grandmother preparing an ofrenda for the late grandfather, and local vendors, bakers and artisans. Activities: build an ofrenda, make pan de muerto, cut papel picado, decorate a sugar skull, visit a cemetery vigil, write a calaverita, learn the history.*

Compiled 2026-10-29.

## 1. The most common ways media, products and schools get it wrong

### 1.1 Treating it as "Mexican Halloween" or framing it as spooky
- Halloween is about scaring spirits away, while Day of the Dead is about inviting them back. Source: KUNC, an NPR member station. https://www.kunc.org/arts-life/2018-11-01/not-mexican-halloween-day-of-the-dead-events-put-spotlight-on-culture
- Learning for Justice (Lauryn Mascareñaz, 27 Oct 2017) argues the holiday is distinctly different from Halloween "in origin, celebration and culture." It says the holiday combines Indigenous rituals with Catholicism: families clean and decorate graves, and those far from graves build an ofrenda at home. It calls the holiday "a wonderful opportunity to foster empathy among students." https://www.learningforjustice.org/magazine/let-dia-de-los-muertos-stand-on-its-own. Learning for Justice also has a grades 9–12 learning plan comparing the two holidays: https://www.learningforjustice.org/learning-plan/day-of-the-dead-vs-halloween
- St. Louis Public Radio reports that using sugar-skull imagery as Halloween face paint strips it of context. A local scholar warned this reinforces stereotypes and reduces a rich tradition to something trivial. https://www.stlpr.org/arts/2016-10-31/no-dia-de-los-muertos-isnt-mexican-halloween
- The Mexican outlet Expansión notes in a 2017 opinion piece that many people in the US confuse the holiday with Halloween. It says the holiday is not supposed to be dark or frightening ("tenebroso o aterrador"). https://expansion.mx/opinion/2017/12/01/opinion-coco-es-la-pelicula-que-los-latinos-en-eu-estaban-esperando
- A Mexico News Daily columnist advises against combining skull face paint with Halloween elements such as pumpkins or spider webs. https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexico-living/is-it-ok-for-foreigners-to-paint-their-faces-on-dia-de-muertos
- **Nuance.** Mathew Sandoval is an Arizona State University cultural historian who writes for The Conversation, and coverage identifies him as Mexican-American. He argues that "Día de los Muertos is not Mexican Halloween" is partly a protective rhetorical strategy. Mexico's tourism industry used it in the 1970s and 1980s, and Latinos used it to push back against readings of skull imagery as witchcraft. He also argues that the two holidays are now blending into a hybrid. Halloween spread in Mexico after NAFTA, and some Mexicans saw it as cultural imperialism. Halloween candy and decorations now appear next to Día de Muertos goods in Mexican stores, and even on ofrendas. Sources: https://localnewsmatters.org/2023/11/01/a-cultural-collision-is-underway-as-halloween-traditions-transform-sacred-day-of-the-dead/ ; https://smdp.com/day-of-the-dead-is-taking-on-halloween-traditions/ ; https://religionnews.com/?p=4116727 ; PBS NewsHour reprint: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/arts/what-is-dia-de-los-muertos-an-expert-explains-the-holiday-celebrating-loved-ones-who-have-died
- Mexican children traditionally *pedir calaverita*: they ask neighbors for sweets or coins with "¿me da mi calaverita?", mainly on 1 November. Mexican outlets describe the custom as rooted in colonial-era or 18th-century practice, not as a copy of Halloween, though it now mixes with Halloween costumes on 31 October. Sources: https://www.excelsior.com.mx/estilo-de-vida/por-que-los-ninos-piden-calaverita-en-mexico-descubre-esta-tradicion-del-dia-de ; https://www.nmas.com.mx/sociedad/que-dia-se-piden-dulces-en-mexico-en-esta-fecha-se-da-calaverita-a-los-ninos-en-2026/
- **[I]** Keep Halloween iconography out of all Día de Muertos content: jack-o'-lanterns, witches, bats, cobwebs, sheet ghosts, haunted-house music and jump scares. If the game shows modern Mexican life honestly, for example kids in costumes asking for their *calaverita*, present it as a modern neighborhood custom and keep it separate from the ofrenda and cemetery scenes.

### 1.2 Costumes, "sexy Catrina" and horror makeup
- Refinery29 (2016) quotes a Virginia Commonwealth University religious-studies professor. La Catrina was created "as a satirical figure to mock the Eurocentric Mexican elite," and "It's a sacred family holiday, so when people try to sexualize La Catrina, it's offensive." https://www.refinery29.com/2016/10/128083/sugar-skulls-halloween-makeup-jodie-sweetin-ashley-tisdale?geo=us
- Detroit Metro Times: "When this symbol of cultural pride is lumped together with the costumes of sexy cats, police women, and call girls, it robs from its beauty." https://www.metrotimes.com/arts/how-to-celebrate-dia-de-los-muertos-without-being-an-appropriating-asshole-2469707/
- Bustle (2018) carries a Mexican-American writer's argument that reducing La Catrina to a "sugar skull" or a "sexy señorita" undermines her significance. https://www.bustle.com/p/wearing-racist-halloween-costumes-was-never-ok-but-in-2018-its-even-bigger-issue-12207595
- Remezcla interviewed Latino attendees at Hollywood Forever Cemetery. One said "some people think it's a costume, but it's not." Another said "sometimes I see people just doing the make-up and not knowing the background or honoring the people." https://remezcla.com/features/culture/dia-de-muertos-celebrations-getting-co-opted-us/
- Counterpoint: the Latin Times reports that sexy Catrina looks are popular with celebrities and in Mexico itself, which complicates any simple rule. https://www.latintimes.com/la-catrina-skeleton-lady-gets-star-treatment-mexican-day-dead-dia-de-los-muertos-132463
- Origin. José Guadalupe Posada made the skeleton etching around 1910–1913 as a satire of people who hid their Indigenous roots to imitate European elites. Diego Rivera's 1947 mural gave her a full body and popularized the name "Catrina." Sources: https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/english/mexican-catrina-evolving-tradition ; https://www.wikiart.org/en/jose-guadalupe-posada/la-calavera-catrina ; Mitú describes her as "Posada's satire of rich women in pre-Revolutionary Mexico": https://wearemitu.com/wearemitu/culture/dia-de-los-muertos-america-commercialization/
- **[I]** Any Catrina or Catrín character should be elegant, dignified, and funny in Posada's spirit, as a joke about vanity and social climbing. Never sexualize her, never make her gory, and never offer her as a Halloween dress-up costume.

### 1.3 Using it as a party theme or merchandise hook, including Disney's 2013 trademark attempt
- **Disney, 2013.** On 1 May 2013 Disney filed to trademark "Día de los Muertos" for a Pixar film across merchandise such as toys, games, clothing, backpacks and jewelry. Latinos pushed back fast on social media. Lalo Alcaraz, a Mexican-American cartoonist, drew a giant skeletal "Muerto Mouse" captioned "It's coming to trademark your cultura." A Change.org petition passed 21,000 signatures within days. Disney withdrew the filing, saying the film's title would change. Pixar later hired Alcaraz, the playwright Octavio Solis and Marcela Davison Avilés, president of the Mexican Heritage Corp., as cultural consultants on *Coco*. Sources: https://edition.cnn.com/2013/05/10/us/disney-trademark-day-dead ; https://www.kpbs.org/news/2013/may/07/after-outcry-disney-withdraws-effort-trademark-dia ; https://www.commondreams.org/news/2013/05/09/corporate-fail-disneys-attempt-trademark-cultural-holiday ; https://remezcla.com/features/film/coco-disney-pixar-trademark/ ; https://www.nbcnews.com/news/latino/cartoonist-lalo-alcaraz-work-pixars-coco-n413771 ; https://wearemitu.com/wearemitu/entertainment/throwback-remember-when-disney-tried-to-trademark-dia-de-los-muertos/
- **Mass retail.** The LA Times (James F. Peltz, 30 Oct 2017) reported that Target, Walmart and other big retailers "plastered the theme on masks, paper plates and candle holders," and that Party City and Spirit Halloween sold costumes. The holiday was being "rolled up into the Halloween retail juggernaut," which unsettled people who saw it as appropriation. The article credits Chicano artists in East LA with popularizing the US celebration from the 1970s. Source (PDF hosted by UCLA Chicano Studies): https://www.chicano.ucla.edu/files/news/LATimes_DiadeLosMuertos_103017.pdf
- A Mitú op-ed lists Stanley cups, Mattel's $75 Day of the Dead Barbie from 2019, and do-it-yourself ofrenda kits at Target and Walmart. It cites Sehila Mota Casper of Latinos in Heritage Conservation, speaking to the AP, and compares the trend to the commercialization of Cinco de Mayo. https://wearemitu.com/wearemitu/culture/dia-de-los-muertos-america-commercialization/
- **The 2019 Barbie split opinion.** Radio host Mariluz Gonzalez called it "obviously cultural appropriation." Its designer, the Mexican-American Javier Meabe, said it was made with "love and respect." Some Latinos welcomed the visibility. Columbia historian Pablo Piccato said the holiday has "been the object of many changes and reappropriations." Sources: https://www.thenationalnews.com/lifestyle/dia-de-los-muertos-day-of-the-dead-barbie-divides-opinion-amid-claims-of-cultural-appropriation-1.909582 ; https://hiplatina.com/dia-de-los-muertos-barbie/
- **Hollywood invented a "tradition."** The big Mexico City parade copies the opening of the James Bond film *Spectre* (2015). It was staged as part of a government campaign to attract tourists. Sources: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/james-bonds-spectre-inspires-dia-de-los-muertos-parade-mexico-city ; https://wearemitu.com/wearemitu/culture/dia-de-los-muertos-james-bond-mexico-city/
- A Remezcla op-ed describes the writer's mixed feelings at a Target Día de Muertos display. It asks whether the community understands its own holiday well enough to protect it. https://remezcla.com/features/culture/op-ed-how-do-we-protect-dia-de-muertos-if-we-dont-understand-it-ourselves/
- **Giving back.** Mitú reported that Disney shared none of *Coco*'s profits with Santa Fe de la Laguna, Michoacán, or with the family of María Salud Ramírez Caballero, the woman widely described as Mamá Coco's inspiration. https://wearemitu.com/wearemitu/culture/mama-coco-disney-maria-salud-dies/
- **Mexico treats unauthorized commercial use of Indigenous designs as a real harm.** The Culture Ministry has sent public letters to brands over Indigenous motifs: Zara, Anthropologie and Patowl in 2021, and SHEIN in 2022 over a Maya floral design. https://www.yahoo.com/lifestyle/mexico-questions-zara-anthropologie-patowi-183338948.html ; https://hiplatina.com/zara-anthropologie-cultural-appropriation/ ; https://briefly.co.za/business-economy/132876-mexico-complains-chinas-shein-mayan-motif/. A federal law published on 17 January 2022 protects the collective cultural heritage of Indigenous and Afro-Mexican peoples and requires communities' free, prior and informed consent for its use or commercialization. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Law_for_the_Protection_of_Cultural_Heritage_of_Indigenous_and_Afro-Mexican_Peoples_and_Communities ; https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/21258
- **[I]** Do not trademark the holiday's name or any of its common symbols. Do not copy a specific community's textile or embroidery pattern onto characters or merchandise. Do not stage the game as a "fiesta theme" party. Have counsel review any merchandise plans against the 2022 law.

### 1.4 Stereotypes: sombreros, ponchos, mustaches, the "sleeping Mexican", tequila jokes, mariachi-everything, the yellow filter
- ClaroSports, a Mexican outlet, asks whether Mexican-inspired games are homage or stereotype "que roza el racismo" ("bordering on racism"). Its list of recurring elements: luchadores, pre-Hispanic cultures, Día de Muertos, the "Mexican western", sombreros, cacti and mariachis. It notes that Mexico is a large games market with few games made there. https://www.clarosports.com/esports/mexico-en-los-videojuegos-la-representacion-mexicana-en-el-gaming-es-un-homenaje-racismo-o-estereotipo/
- A 2023 Drexel thesis, "Digital Mexican: Visual Representation in Video Games," concludes that Mexican culture in games is often stereotyped, underrepresented or appropriated. https://researchdiscovery.drexel.edu/esploro/outputs/graduate/Digital-Mexican-visual-representation-in-video/991020879214104721
- A 2025 peer-reviewed article finds that popular games tie Mexican and Mexican-American characters to criminality and lean on Catholic iconography as shorthand. https://link.springer.com/article/10.1057/s41276-025-00532-3
- *Super Mario Odyssey*'s sombrero-and-poncho Mario and its Día de Muertos-inspired desert world drew criticism. Critic Julie Muncy called the world "a clumsy mashup of Latin American and Pacific Islander stereotypes," and an Unwinnable essay argued the sombrero outfit caricatures a stereotype (https://unwinnable.com/2017/10/30/video-game-journos-and-giving-mario-a-pass/). Nintendo replaced the sombrero image on the box art (https://www.xataka.com.mx/videojuegos/nintendo-elimina-al-mario-mexicano-de-la-portada-de-super-mario-odyssey). Spanish-language coverage noted that many Mexican fans were excited to see their culture in the game. Reactions were mixed, and the coverage reflects fan comments, not polling.
- Latino USA's episode "Stereotyped" (2016) covers the "Sleeping Mexican" figure, which is widely read as mocking Mexicans as lazy or drunk. https://www.npr.org/2016/03/18/470989737/-1612-stereotyped ; https://www.latinousa.org/2016/03/18/the-sleeping-mexican-honored-cultural-icon-or-derogatory-stereotype/ ; https://tostadamagazine.com/2018/04/04/this-restaurant-advertised-with-a-lazy-mexican-trope-and-these-detroiters-were-not-having-it/
- **The "Mexican filter."** Film and TV often give Mexico a yellow or sepia color grade. Critics say it stereotypes the country and washes out darker skin. In 2023 the Mexican cinematographer Emmanuel Lubezki appeared in Corona's "Unfilter México" campaign urging filmmakers to drop the look. https://www.denofgeek.com/tv/15-times-american-movies-pretended-mexico-was-yellow/ ; https://shots.net/news/view/coronas-view-on-local-colour ; https://wearemitu.com/?p=273468
- **"Mexican Hat Dance."** The Jarabe Tapatío is Mexico's national dance, a courtship dance from Jalisco. Its tune is reused as a lazy "Mexico" cue, for example for *The Simpsons*' stereotyped Bumblebee Man. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexican_hat_dance
- **Drinking.** Michoacán's Consejo Supremo Indígena has asked visitors not to drink alcohol during the celebrations and not to step on altars and tombs, after locals complained of drinking at cemetery entrances and interrupted vigils (Excélsior, 2023). https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/denuncian-falta-respeto-turistas-durante-dia-de-muertos-michoacan/1617537. In Teotitlán del Valle, Oaxaca, a local described tour buses unloading visitors looking for mezcal shots and pointing cameras at people without asking. https://oaxacaculture.com/tag/respect
- **[I]** No sombrero-and-serape shorthand, no big-mustache vendors, no sleeping figure under a cactus, no drunk or tequila gags, no mariachi stingers on every screen, and no yellow color grade.

### 1.5 Mixing in things that don't belong
- Halloween items. See 1.1.
- **Alebrijes.** A Latinx communication-studies zine from Willamette University notes that alebrijes are not typically part of Día de Muertos. *Coco* used them for their color and Mexican origin. https://willamette.edu/cla/ccm/experiential-learning/latinx-communication-studies/zines-pdf/liz-stewart.pdf. **[GK]** Alebrijes are usually credited to Pedro Linares in 1930s Mexico City.
- **Mixing cultures.** *The Book of Life* names its underworld ruler Xibalba, which is the K'iche' Maya name for the underworld (https://thefairytaletraveler.com/2014/10/08/the-book-of-life). **[I]** If the game mentions Mesoamerican beliefs, attribute each one to a specific people, such as the Mexica (Aztecs), the Maya or the Purépecha. Do not blend them into one generic "ancient Mexican" religion.
- **Contested or recent "rules" repeated online.** The "seven levels" of an altar and its fixed meanings are popular interpretations, and blogs contradict each other (see Section 5). The pet ofrenda on 27 October is described by Mexican outlets as a recent, unofficial custom that spread through social media. https://www.aztecajalisco.com/tendenciayviral/cuando-regresan-las-mascotas-del-mas-alla-esta-es-la-fecha-para-poner-su-ofrenda/ ; https://www.cronica.com.mx/tendencias/2025/10/06/ofrenda-para-mascotas-como-y-cuando-armar-el-altar-para-perros-y-gatos-fallecidos-este-dia-de-muertos-2025/ . Remezcla covers how popular pet ofrendas have become online: https://remezcla.com/lists/culture/pet-ofrendas-dia-de-muertos-tik-tok/
- **[GK]** Piñatas belong to posadas and birthdays, not to Día de Muertos. Keep them out.

### 1.6 Misspelling and mispronouncing
- Even a public-radio page was published as "Dia de los **Muertes**" (KNPR, 2010, an interview with Gustavo Arellano). https://knpr.org/knpr/2010-11/dia-de-los-muertes
- **[GK]** Common errors: "Dia" without the accent; "Muertes"; "Day of Death" or "Día de la Muerte" (the holiday is about the dead, not death); "Dia de Los Muertos" with a capital "Los" in Spanish; "pan de muertos" (the usual form is *pan de muerto*); "cempasuchil" without the accent; "Xolo-itz-quintli" spellings. See Section 6.

### 1.7 Flattening regional and Indigenous diversity, and assuming every Mexican celebrates
- UNESCO's listing is titled **"Indigenous festivity dedicated to the dead"** (proclaimed 2003, inscribed 2008). It describes Indigenous communities in Mexico welcoming the temporary return of deceased relatives at the end of the maize cycle. Petals, candles and offerings guide the souls, and favorite foods are placed on altars and graves. The dead are grouped by cause of death, age, sex and sometimes profession, and each group has its own day. https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/indigenous-festivity-dedicated-to-the-dead-00054
- Mexico's national institute of anthropology and history (INAH) describes the practice as a blend of Indigenous, Catholic and other influences. It says that in Maya, Nahua, Zapotec and Mixtec regions these practices are fundamental to each people's identity and worldview. https://lugares.inah.gob.mx/en/node/6394
- Regional examples. Most of these come from regional news and travel sources, so treat them as pointers for a consultant.
  - **Hanal Pixán** ("food for the souls") among the Maya of the Yucatán Peninsula, with *mucbipollo* (a large baked tamal) and a green cross representing the ceiba tree: https://midcitybeat.com/foodie/hanal-pixan-and-day-of-the-dead/
  - **Pomuch, Campeche**: families clean and re-wrap their relatives' bones in the *Choo Ba'ak* ritual: https://www.mexicotravelandleisure.com/blog/pomuch-day-of-the-dead/
  - **Janitzio, Michoacán**: Purépecha candlelit canoes and an all-night vigil: https://planoinformativo.com/1173900/los-mejores-lugares-de-mexico-para-vivir-el-dia-de-muertos
  - **Mixquic, Mexico City**: the cemetery *Alumbrada* with thousands of candles: https://www.chilango.com/ocio/dia-de-muertos-en-mixquic/
  - **Xantolo** in the Huasteca, and **muerteadas/comparsas** and Zapotec altar forms in Oaxaca: https://www.excelsior.com.mx/cultura/fiestas-indigenas-dedicadas-muertos-cuales-son-y-donde-hacen ; https://www.unotv.com/nacional/dia-de-muertos-2026-estas-son-las-tradiciones-en-estados-de-mexico/
- **Not everyone celebrates.** A Mexican writer notes that traditions vary from state to state and that some parts of Mexico don't celebrate at all (https://mexicoinmypocket.com/blogs/blog/how-to-build-a-day-of-the-dead-altar). Many evangelical Christians in Mexico do not take part, because they find no biblical basis for it (https://www.informabtl.com/?p=350961). The claim that northern Mexico historically did not celebrate appears only in an uncited blog, so it is unverified.
- **Indigenous erasure in *Coco*.** A Latino Rebels essay by Eren Cervantes-Altamirano asks where the Indigenous peoples are in the film. It links the omission to Mexican nation-building around *mestizaje*, the idea of a single mixed national identity. https://latinorebels.com/?p=45778
- **[I]** Set the fictional town in a specific region and let its customs come from there. Have characters say things like "in our town we…" and mention that other regions do it differently. Show Indigenous people as living, present-day neighbors, not only as "ancient Aztecs."

## 2. Teaching it respectfully to children

### 2.1 Framing: love, memory, family, a celebration of life without denying grief
- Cesáreo Moreno, chief curator of the National Museum of Mexican Art in Chicago: "Even though it's called Day of the Dead, it is actually a celebration of life" (WTTW, 2015), https://news.wttw.com/2015/10/16/day-dead-comes-life-national-museum-mexican-art. Speaking to NPR in 2020, he called it a way of coping with and understanding the universal idea of death: https://www.npr.org/transcripts/929938826
- Mathew Sandoval: "The beautiful thing about Día de los Muertos is that it allows the pain and difficulty [of death] to be transformed through joyous celebration." (PBS NewsHour, via The Conversation, 2023) https://www.pbs.org/newshour/arts/what-is-dia-de-los-muertos-an-expert-explains-the-holiday-celebrating-loved-ones-who-have-died
- NBC News Latino (2023):
  - Thanatology psychologist Alicia Hinojosa says that when adults treat death as a natural part of life, children develop less fear and fewer taboos.
  - Thanatologist Regina Cantú says the body dies but memories stay alive.
  - Writer and linguist Rossy Lima calls the holiday a kind of surprise party for loved ones who died before us.
  Source: https://www.nbcnews.com/news/latino/coco-day-dead-help-kids-families-death-grief-rcna123165
- Mexican director Jorge R. Gutiérrez (*The Book of Life*) described the holiday as "a very positive thing. It's about joyfully remembering the people no longer with us." (2014 press preview, via search excerpt.) https://animatedviews.com/2014/first-look-at-the-book-of-life/
- The AP (2025) calls it "traditionally an intimate family affair, observed with home altars — ofrendas — and visits to the cemetery." https://www.mprnews.org/story/2025/11/01/mexican-americans-balance-tradition-and-modernity-in-day-of-the-dead-celebrations
- A Catholic school principal writes that it is not a day when the dead are worshipped but one of remembrance, and that the ofrenda expresses love and honor. https://nceatalk.org/?p=18697
- **Caveat.** The anthropologist Claudio Lomnitz shows that a joking intimacy with death became a cornerstone of *national* identity in the 20th century (https://www.zonebooks.org/books/35-death-and-the-idea-of-mexico). He also notes that attitudes toward death are not uniform across Mexico (cited in https://mediacommons.org/imr/content/small-traces-death-positivity-el-laberinto-del-fauno-pan%E2%80%99s-labyrinth). Ofrendas also carry real grief and social memory. The National Museum of Mexican Art's 2023 exhibition honored earthquake victims and murdered or disappeared women: https://chicago.suntimes.com/entertainment-and-culture/2023/9/26/23885549/day-of-the-dead-exhibit-national-museum-of-mexican-art-pilsen
- **[I]** Frame the game around love, memory, family stories and celebration. Let the grandmother be openly sad sometimes, and let her joy sit alongside that sadness. Avoid the cliché that "Mexicans aren't afraid of death" or "laugh at death."

### 2.2 How teachers handle the ofrenda activity
- **Who to honor.**
  - The Learning for Justice article, as reprinted on ShareMyLesson, suggests remembering "a grandparent, friend, even a pet" through writing or a classroom altar: https://sharemylesson.com/blog/let-dia-de-los-muertos-stand-its-own
  - TeachersFirst (2025) suggests a classroom altar where students contribute items for ancestors, loved ones or pets: https://teachersfirst.org/blog/2025/10/day-of-the-dead-building-family-connections-through-cultural-celebration/
  - The Columbus Museum's ofrenda-box project uses a photo of a person or a pet: https://www.columbusmuseum.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/OfrendaBoxProject_8.5x11.pdf
  - Classroom guides offer famous or historical figures, animals or fictional characters, noting the altar "doesn't have to be personal" if students aren't comfortable: https://www.hmhco.com/blog/dia-de-los-muertos-day-of-the-dead-activities-for-students ; https://kimberlysclassroom.com/how-to-celebrate-day-of-the-dead-in-your-classroom/
  - In Mexico, the federal education ministry's (SEP) 2026 national calaverita contest asks students to write about **figures from Mexican history** (see 7.6).
  - Public ofrendas honor community figures too. The San Diego History Center's altar included Bum, the city's famous 19th-century town dog: https://coolsandiegosights.com/2025/10/03/beautiful-ofrenda-at-san-diego-history-center
- **Grieving children.**
  - A Psychology Today column for schools ("Supporting Children Through Crisis and Loss", 2018) says the holiday can act as a grief trigger. Teachers should talk to a grieving student ahead of time, describe the activity, and offer an alternative. Because teachers can't know every family's history, they should offer choices to all students. https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/supporting-children-through-crisis-and-loss/201810/day-of-the-dead-dia-de-los-muertos ; see also https://www.schoolcrisiscenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Day-of-the-Dead.pdf and https://schoolcrisishealing.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/SCRR-x-DDLM-2021.pdf
  - A guide for child-care providers recommends several low-pressure ways to contribute, such as digital photos, notes or pre-made items: https://www.childcareed.com/a/how-can-child-care-providers-teach-d-a-de-los-muertos-with-cultural-sensitivity.html
  - Grief-support activities: https://elunanetwork.org/resources/dia-de-los-muertos-day-of-the-dead-grief-support-activities/
- **Families of other faiths, and opt-outs.**
  - In fall 2025 a first-grade class at Broadway Elementary in Los Angeles was required to build an ofrenda, and a parent said the family did not share those beliefs. After the Freedom From Religion Foundation (FFRF) wrote to the district, LAUSD's counsel said students may opt out and must be given an alternate activity. Any religious themes must be "presented in a neutral and objective manner." This account comes from FFRF's own write-up: https://ffrf.org/legal/other-legal-successes/ffrf-pointed-out-secular-students-rights-not-to-participate-in-religious-assignments-march-2026/
  - Comprehensible Classroom provides a letter to send home to caretakers, plus monarch-butterfly alternative lessons for faith-based or trauma-based opt-outs: https://comprehensibleclassroom.com/2022/10/16/dia-de-muertos-caretaker-letter ; https://comprehensibleclassroom.com/2020/10/18/teaching-about-cultural-traditions-holidays/
  - Spanish Mama suggests an essay about the person instead of building an altar: https://spanishmama.com/?p=37437
- **[I] Applying this to the game.**
  - The story already supplies the honoree, Abuelo, so no player has to use a real loss.
  - Make any "your own ofrenda" mode optional, and offer: a grandparent or relative, a pet, a historical figure, someone you admire, an ancestor you'd like to learn about, or a fictional character.
  - Don't ask kids to upload photos of real people. Let them draw or choose icons instead. **[GK]** This also avoids collecting children's data, an issue under COPPA.
  - Add a gentle line such as "It's OK to feel sad, or to feel nothing at all. You can talk to a grown-up you trust."
  - Never require the player to pray or to affirm a belief.

### 2.3 Is it OK for non-Mexicans to make an ofrenda?
Most sources from the community welcome respectful participation. A minority push back.
- **Welcoming.**
  - Remezcla interviewee Maria Perez: "as long as people embrace and respect Día de Muertos and do it because they actually feel that way, then I think it's something that anyone can celebrate." https://remezcla.com/features/culture/dia-de-muertos-celebrations-getting-co-opted-us/
  - The AP (2025) reports that advocates say it's OK for celebrations to evolve "as long as their core meaning remains intact," and that Moreno says *Coco* gave people outside the community a way to learn about the tradition: https://www.mprnews.org/story/2025/11/01/mexican-americans-balance-tradition-and-modernity-in-day-of-the-dead-celebrations
  - NPR (2024): at Raleigh's Día de Oakwood, people who had never celebrated began leaving photos of their dead on the organizer's ofrenda: https://www.npr.org/2024/11/01/nx-s1-5172608/at-a-historic-cemetery-in-the-south-a-dia-de-los-muertos-festival-takes-root
  - A Tulane opinion piece calls fear of appropriation over a private ofrenda unfounded and treats corporate use as a separate issue: https://tulanehullabaloo.com/57764/intersections/opinion-who-owns-dia-de-los-muertos/
  - A shop owner interviewed by Parade has no problem with people respectfully making their own: https://parade.com/living/ofrenda
  - US parishes without Mexican heritage have adopted ofrendas: https://catholicmessenger.net/?p=45749
- **Cautious.**
  - Andi Xoch, a Latinx plant seller in East LA, told NPR she worries about "the borderline or sometimes outright cultural appropriation of what was once an indigenous holiday." She says non-Latinx people walk a fine line, especially with calavera face paint and Catrina costumes. She welcomes young Latinx people embracing the tradition, and, per a search excerpt, called cempasúchil displays a non-offensive way for anyone to take part. https://www.npr.org/2021/10/30/1050726374/why-marigolds-or-cempasuchil-are-the-iconic-flower-of-dia-de-los-muertos
  - A Mexico-based intercultural blog says most Latinos it heard from are happy to share but resent outsiders "transforming them into something they're not": https://blog.culturaldetective.com/2015/10/27/cultural-appropriation-of-day-of-the-dead/
- **Critical.** Aya de Leon, a writer of Puerto Rican heritage, argues in "Dear White People/Queridos Gringos: … Stop Colonizing the Day of the Dead" that the community's welcome has been abused. https://newstaco.com/2015/11/02/dear-white-peoplequeridos-gringos-you-want-our-culture-but-you-dont-want-us-stop-colonizing-the-day-of-the-dead/
- **Common advice for outsiders.** Learn the meaning, go to events run by Mexican and Mexican-American communities, and buy from Latino-owned vendors and artisans. Sources: https://wdet.org/2016/11/02/how-to-celebrate-dia-de-los-muertos-a-brief-guide-for-non-latinos/ ; https://talkdeath.com/cultural-appropriation-how-not-celebrate-day-of-the-dead ; https://www.latinpost.com/articles/161382/20231103/three-steps-to-respectfully-celebrating-dia-de-los-muertos-as-an-outsider.htm
- **[I]** Welcome all players. The player joins a Mexican family's tradition as a guest or family member and learns why each thing is done. The game shouldn't imply that players are now "doing Día de Muertos correctly" or that anyone owns it.

### 2.4 How to credit Indigenous origins without over-claiming
- **Indigenous roots are real and recognized.** UNESCO frames the holiday as Indigenous (see 1.7). A Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian blog (2020) says its roots are "millennia-deep in Indigenous Mexico." NMAI posts from that month mention rituals for the Mexica goddess Mictecacíhuatl and note that Olmec, Maya and Toltec peoples also made offerings. https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/national-museum-american-indian/2020/10/29/day-dead-2020-online/ ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/national-museum-american-indian/2020/10/
- **But historians disagree on how much is Indigenous and how much is Spanish Catholic.**
  - Mexican historian Elsa Malvido traced most of the custom, including skull-shaped sweets and bone-shaped bread, to medieval Spain.
  - INAH archaeologist Víctor Joel Santos Ramírez argues it is not, strictly speaking, pre-Hispanic. His INAH colleague Orlando Casares estimates it is about 80–90% European in origin. Sources: https://www.aciprensa.com/noticias/101841/el-origen-del-dia-de-muertos-es-cristiano ; https://expansion.mx/tendencias/2025/10/31/dia-de-muertos-origen-2-de-noviembre ; https://www.yucatan.com.mx/merida/2024/10/31/dia-de-muertos-de-origen-europeo-adoptado-en-mexico-no-prehispanico.html
  - Mexican columnist Veka Duncan questions a purely pre-Hispanic origin while acknowledging that Indigenous peoples did have a cult of the dead: https://en.opinion51.com/veka-dunkan-2411-muertos-no
  - Most of what we know about Mexica death rituals comes from Spanish friars (Durán and Sahagún), whose accounts conflict: https://glasstire.com/2019/10/31/is-day-of-the-dead-more-indigenous-or-catholic-friars-duran-and-sahagun-vs-wikipedia/ ; https://sanantonioreport.org/a-brief-history-of-day-of-the-dead/
  - Britannica gives the mainstream synthesis: Mesoamerican beliefs blended with All Saints' and All Souls' Days. https://www.britannica.com/topic/Day-of-the-Dead
- **[I] Suggested in-game wording to have a consultant check:** "Long before the Spanish arrived, many Indigenous peoples in what is now Mexico honored their dead with offerings. After the Spanish conquest, those ways mixed with the Catholic feasts of All Saints and All Souls. Historians still debate which parts came from where. Today every region, town and family celebrates in its own way, including many Indigenous communities like the Purépecha, Maya, Nahua, Zapotec and Mixtec." Avoid flat claims like "a 3,000-year-old Aztec festival for the goddess Mictecacíhuatl."

## 3. Face painting and Catrina makeup

Sources mostly agree on a middle position. Painting your face as a calavera is broadly accepted, including for non-Mexicans, **when it honors the holiday in its own context**. It is objected to when it becomes a Halloween costume, horror makeup, a sexualized look, or a product sold for profit.
- **Welcomed with respect.**
  - The Mexico News Daily columnist says anyone, "be it Mexican or foreign," can paint their face as a skull, but should not do it as a Halloween costume or alongside pumpkins and spider webs: https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexico-living/is-it-ok-for-foreigners-to-paint-their-faces-on-dia-de-muertos
  - Refinery29 (2019) asked Mexican and Latina beauty voices whether it's appropriation and got "For the most part: no." Regina Merson says it's fine with good intentions and respect for the holiday. Yasmin Maya: "anyone can do sugar skull makeup, but they must honor the holiday and understand the meaning behind it." https://refinery29.com/en-ca/2019/10/8661966/read-this-before-doing-sugar-skull-makeup
  - A Mexico-based blog says most locals are delighted to share: https://mexlocal.com/?p=1837
  - Bustle's Melanie Mignucci says it isn't necessarily appropriation, but wearers should recognize the culture they draw on: https://www.bustle.com/p/dia-de-los-muertos-has-nothing-to-do-with-halloween-heres-what-else-americans-should-know-about-it-13013148
  - Mitú publishes Catrín makeup tutorials for men, showing the look is embraced inside the community: https://wearemitu.com/wearemitu/culture/dia-de-muertos-makeup-tutorials-for-men/
- **Objected to.**
  - Merson calls horror-style sugar-skull makeup "one of the most offensive things" (Refinery29, above).
  - Sexualized Catrina costumes draw objection (see 1.2).
  - Xoch's warning about calavera face paint and Catrina costumes (NPR, above).
  - Remezcla interviewees object to people "just doing the make-up and not knowing the background or honoring the people" (above).
  - A State Hornet student op-ed is titled "Stop using my culture as your costume": https://statehornet.com/2019/11/opinion-dia-de-los-muertos/
- **[I] For the game.** Sugar-skull *decorating* is a genuine Mexican craft and is fine (see 7.7). If face painting appears, keep it inside a celebration in the Mexican town, such as a neighborhood Catrina parade or contest, where a local character explains why people do it: to honor and joke with death, and to remember. Keep it cosmetic and optional. No gore, no "scary" makeup, no sexualized styling, no Halloween tie-in.

## 4. Showing the returning dead and skeleton characters

### 4.1 What the tradition itself says about the visiting souls
- Families guide the souls home with petals, candles and offerings for a temporary visit (UNESCO, above).
- The scent of cempasúchil (marigold) is said to lead souls from their burial place to their family homes (NPR): https://www.npr.org/2021/10/30/1050726374/why-marigolds-or-cempasuchil-are-the-iconic-flower-of-dia-de-los-muertos
- Mexico City's government page and Mexican outlets give these meanings for ofrenda items. Water was offered because the dead were thought to arrive thirsty after a long journey, and today it also stands for purification. Salt purifies. Copal clears the space of evil spirits and lifts prayers. The marigold's color and smell guide the dead to the altar. All add that contents vary by family and region. https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/day-of-the-dead-ofrendas/ ; https://www.unotv.com/cultura/ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos-ve-el-significado-de-sus-elementos/ ; https://politica.expansion.mx/sociedad/2025/10/15/que-debe-llevar-una-ofrenda-del-dia-de-muertos-significado
- **Monarch butterflies.** In Michoacán and the State of Mexico, Indigenous traditions (attributed variously to the Purépecha and the Mazahua) link the monarchs' arrival to returning ancestral souls. Sources: https://www.nwf.org/Magazines/National-Wildlife/2024/Fall/Conservation/Monarch-Migration-Mexico ; https://monarchjointventure.org/blog/monarchs-and-dia-de-muertos-in-mexico ; Smithsonian Folklife: https://folklife.si.edu/Contents/Item/Display/15645
- **Xoloitzcuintle.** The Xolo was believed to accompany souls through the nine levels of Mictlán and help them cross a river. Ceramic Xolo figures found in tombs date back more than 3,000 years. Sources: AKC Museum of the Dog, https://museumofthedog.org/xoloitzcuintle/ ; Museo Dolores Olmedo via Google Arts & Culture, https://Artsandculture.google.com/story/3wVBKHc2YeLpKg ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/xoloitzcuintle-acompanante-fiel-hasta-la-muerte.html
- **Children's souls** are commonly said to arrive on 31 October or 1 November and adults' on 2 November. Mexican outlets stress that dates vary between communities: https://www.aztecajalisco.com/arte-y-cultura/dia-de-muertos-2026-que-almas-llegan-del-27-de-octubre-al-2-de-noviembre/ ; https://www.guiainfantil.com/educacion/muerte/cuando-llegan-los-ninos-difuntos-a-los-altares-por-el-dia-de-muertos/

### 4.2 *Coco* (Pixar, 2017): how it was made, what it was praised for, what it was criticized for
- **Process.** Lee Unkrich says the team spent three years visiting museums, markets, plazas, workshops, churches, haciendas and cemeteries in Mexico City, Oaxaca and Guanajuato. The Rivera family was based on families they embedded with: https://nofilmschool.com/2017/11/watch-travel-mexico-pixar-research-story-coco. Co-director Adrian Molina, whose mother is from Jalisco, put his own family's experiences into the film: https://www.slashfilm.com/coco-interview/. The consultants changed concrete details, for example swapping Abuelita's wooden spoon for a *chancla* (sandal): https://articles.aplus.com/film-forward/pixar-coco-mexican-culture-consultants
- **Skeleton design.** Pixar gave the skeletons large, expressive eyes, used the eye sockets to suggest eyebrows, and left out gums, tongues and anything that reads as a corpse. Painted calavera faces, wigs and individual clothing made each character a distinct person and kept the film from feeling morbid. https://gizmodo.com/theres-one-thing-pixar-had-to-add-to-make-cocos-skeleto-1820117736 ; https://cinemablend.com/news/1696920/how-coco-turned-the-day-of-the-dead-into-something-entertaining-and-not-morbid ; https://cinemablend.com/news/1696910/the-challenge-cocos-skeletons-created-for-pixar-animators
- **Praise.**
  - Remezcla's Vanessa Erazo: Mexican culture is "treated with dignity, honor, respect, and the utmost reverence. It is not exotic or seen as foreign, in fact it's normalized." https://remezcla.com/lists/film/latino-film-critics-review-pixar-coco/
  - Latino moviegoers praised the accuracy of the family dynamics: https://abc7news.com/coco-latino-culture-hispanic-accuracy-pixar-movies-new/2804665/
  - It opened in Mexico a month before the US, timed to Día de Muertos, and became the highest-grossing film in Mexican history in pesos: https://remezcla.com/film/pixar-coco-highest-grossing-animated-film-mexico-history/
  - Univision found many Mexicans had feared "una cinta llena de clichés" (a film full of clichés) but reacted enthusiastically: https://www.univision.com/entretenimiento/cine-y-series/coco-le-hace-justicia-a-mexico-21-mexicanos-opinan-de-la-nueva-pelicula-de-pixar
  - Common Sense Media noted the "rocky start" with Latino communities and Pixar's course correction: https://www.commonsensemedia.org/movie-reviews/coco
- **Critiques.**
  - Indigenous erasure and mestizo nationalism (Latino Rebels, above).
  - A Spanish-language left-wing outlet (La Izquierda Diario) says the film reproduces a stereotype of the Mexican family, and that a Xolo shown as a street dog is unrealistic: https://www.laizquierdadiario.com/Que-hay-detras-de-Coco
  - Alebrijes are not part of the holiday (Willamette zine, above).
  - Latino critics on a Remezcla podcast said mainstream reviewers were "looking for the otherness." They cited an IndieWire line comparing Mexican culture to a poncho bought at Epcot: https://remezcla.com/film/podcast-coco-reviews/
  - *Coco*'s profits were not shared with the communities that inspired it (Mitú, above).

### 4.3 *The Book of Life* (2014, directed by Jorge R. Gutiérrez, produced by Guillermo del Toro)
- **Praise.** Gutiérrez wanted to show "a hundred of the thousands and thousands of ideas of what a Mexican is" (https://www.cartoonbrew.com/?p=107509). Del Toro: "in order to be universal, we tried to be very specific to our culture" (https://themarysue.com/?p=228228). A review in Dialogos calls it one of the few positive representations of Mexico in Hollywood (https://dialogos.ca/2016/11/mexican-culture-animated-translation/). A Catholic News Service review, as summarized in search results, reads the film as using the holiday to highlight the enduring bonds of family rather than Halloween-party or near-diabolical imagery. Verify the wording against the review: https://www.archbalt.org/movie-review-the-book-of-life/
- **Critiques.** Slant says "no ethnic stereotype is too off-limits" in the film (https://www.slantmagazine.com/film/the-book-of-life). Dialogos says English intrusions in Spanish dialogue make it feel like "a gringo production." One review notes few voice actors of Mexican descent (https://wherever-i-look.com/movies/the-book-of-life-overview-review-with-spoilers). It also mixes Maya (Xibalba) and Mexican imagery (see 1.5).

### 4.4 Design guidance for spirits **[I]**
- Spirits are **beloved, specific people**: Abuelo with his hat, his favorite song, his bad jokes. They are not monsters, zombies, enemies, puzzles or loot.
- Show their arrival through the tradition's own gentle signs: marigold petals stirring along the path, candle flames leaning toward the altar, a monarch landing on the photo, the Xolo's tail thumping at an empty chair, the smell of food "being enjoyed." These signs also respect families who believe in a literal visit and families who see it as remembrance, so the game doesn't impose one doctrine.
- If skeletons appear, use *Coco*'s lessons: big warm eyes, painted calavera faces, individual clothing and personality. No exposed gore, no hollow sockets, no moaning ghosts, no possession, no fog-and-crows horror palette.
- Treat child deaths (*angelitos*, 31 Oct–1 Nov) factually and briefly if at all. Don't make them a plot device.
- Don't present Pixar's "final death" (being forgotten) or *Book of Life*-style afterlife geography as traditional doctrine. They are storytelling inventions. I did not verify their reception in this pass.
- Keep slapstick about real remains out entirely. Pomuch's bone-cleaning is sacred family care, and visitors are told not to ask families to open the boxes (see 1.7).

## 5. Religious sensitivity: Catholic and Indigenous, with no single "true" version

- **Catholic layer.** 1 November is All Saints' Day, which Expansión says Pope Boniface IV instituted in 609. 2 November is All Souls' Day, which it says Odilo of Cluny established in 998. https://expansion.mx/tendencias/2025/10/31/dia-de-muertos-origen-2-de-noviembre. Catholic sources describe ofrendas that may hold crucifixes, saints' images, candles, photos and favorite foods, with prayers for the souls. They frame this as remembrance, not worship. Sources: https://www.catholicnewsagency.com/news/da_de_los_muertos_blends_catholic_tradition_and_latin_american_culture ; https://nceatalk.org/?p=18697 ; https://campusministry.georgetown.edu/?p=1962
- **Indigenous layer.** UNESCO (above). INAH: the practices are fundamental to the identity and worldview of Maya, Nahua, Zapotec and Mixtec peoples. Even "Indigenous" altars often blend in Christian elements, such as the green cross for the ceiba tree in Hanal Pixán. Anthropologist Anya Peterson Royce documents how the Isthmus Zapotec meld Catholic and Indigenous practice: https://www.catholicsandcultures.org/node/545
- **Some Christians object.** Many evangelical Protestants don't participate. Catholics point to 2 Maccabees 12:44–46, which is not in Protestant Bibles, as grounds for praying for the dead: https://www.informabtl.com/?p=350961. Some US parents object on secular or other-faith grounds (the LAUSD case, Section 2.2).
- **There is no single correct ofrenda.**
  - "There is no wrong or right way" (cultural expert Frank Delgado, ABC13): https://abc13.com/post/making-your-own-altar-for-dia-de-los-muertos/2591689/
  - A Mexican author: "not a one-size fits all process": https://mexicoinmypocket.com/blogs/blog/how-to-build-a-day-of-the-dead-altar
  - Mexico News Daily says an altar can be as simple or complex as you like: https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexico-living/how-to-create-an-altar-to-honor-your-departed-on-day-of-the-dead
  - Milenio notes that even the meaning of the "bones" on pan de muerto varies by community: https://amp.milenio.com/cultura/significado-huesitos-pan-muerto
  - Altars have two, three or seven levels by regional and family custom. Interpretations conflict: heaven, earth and underworld; heaven, purgatory and hell; or the Holy Trinity. A UNAM-hosted document reproducing material from Mexico's National Institute of Indigenous Peoples (INPI) gives sky, earth and underworld: https://www.personal.unam.mx/Docs/Cendi/dia-de-muertos-elementos-de-la-ofrenda.pdf ; https://mexicoinmypocket.com/blogs/blog/how-to-build-a-day-of-the-dead-altar
- **[I]**
  - Describe beliefs rather than prescribe them: "Many families believe…", "Abuela says…", "In some towns…".
  - Show the Catholic and Indigenous layers side by side as part of one living practice. Don't rank them as "real" versus "added later."
  - Let the grandmother's ofrenda include a small cross or a Virgin of Guadalupe image if consultants agree it fits the family. Don't make religious items required or scored.
  - Never script the player to pray. A character can pray while the player watches.
  - Give a non-religious path through the game that rests on memory, gratitude and storytelling.

## 6. Language: naming, accents, Spanish usage

- **"Día de Muertos" vs "Día de los Muertos."** Both are correct and both have long histories. Gustavo Arellano's "Ask a Mexican" column says both have been used in Mexico since the 16th century and calls both technically correct, with "Día de Muertos" the literal form of All Souls' Day: https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/news/ask-a-mexican-on-da-de-los-muertos-and-a-pronunciation-lesson-at-taco-bells-drive-thru-6460211/. **[I]** In the Mexican sources gathered for this report, **"Día de Muertos"** is by far the dominant form. Examples: Expansión, Excélsior, UnoTV and México Desconocido headlines, and an INAH study titled *El origen del Día de Muertos* (as reported by https://www.aciprensa.com/noticias/101841/el-origen-del-dia-de-muertos-es-cristiano). US usage, shaped by the Chicano tradition, often says **"Día de los Muertos"** (Smithsonian, NPR, Learning for Justice). A reader letter recalls hearing only "Día de Muertos" in Mexico and only "Día de los Muertos" in the US: https://www.houstonpress.com/?p=286483. **[I]** The National Museum of Mexican Art's recent exhibition titles use "Día de Muertos" (https://nationalmuseumofmexicanart.org/events/opening-reception-dia-de-muertos-memories-and-offerings), while its 1990s titles used "Día de los Muertos" (https://nationalmuseumofmexicanart.org/events/dia-de-los-muertos-1995).
- **English.** "Day of the Dead" is fine. It is not "Day of Death."
- **"Ofrenda" vs "altar."** Moreno notes that *ofrendas* are technically the offerings placed on the altar, though people use the word for the whole altar: https://news.wttw.com/2015/10/16/day-dead-comes-life-national-museum-mexican-art
- **Spanish quality.** Latino critics praised *Coco* for normalizing Mexican culture (above). A reviewer faulted *The Book of Life* for English breaking into Spanish dialogue (above). *Coco*'s Latin American Spanish dub cast was credited for part of its success in Mexico (https://www.slashfilm.com/554556/coco-box-office-mexico/).
- **[I]** Characters in a Mexican town should say "Día de Muertos," "Todos Santos," "los Fieles Difuntos" and "la ofrenda." The English UI can say "Day of the Dead (Día de Muertos)." Always keep accents (Día, cempasúchil, Mictlán, ¡Qué bonito!). Use Mexican Spanish, not Castilian or generic "textbook" Spanish. Hire Mexican writers or editors and Mexican voice actors, and offer a full Mexican-Spanish version. Avoid sprinkling Spanish words for flavor ("Gracias, amigo!"). Use Spanish where a Mexican kid naturally would: family names like Abuela or Abuelita, food names, holiday terms.

## 7. Concrete do's and don'ts for the game

### 7.1 Process: who makes and reviews it
- **What sources show worked.**
  - *Coco*: paid Mexican and Mexican-American consultants whose changes stuck, plus years of research in Mexico (4.2).
  - *Mulaka*, a game about the Rarámuri made by Lienzo, a studio in Chihuahua: co-founder Edgar Serrano told Remezcla every element was checked and approved by anthropologists or cultural leaders, and the studio pledged up to 10% of proceeds to the community: https://remezcla.com/culture/mulaka-raramuri-tarahumara-switch-xbox-ps4/ ; https://www.xataka.com.mx/videojuegos/mulaka-un-videojuego-desarrollado-por-mexicanos-basado-en-el-pueblo-tarahumara
  - *Guacamelee!*: Mexican-born artist Augusto Quijano proposed the theme and chose an agave farmer over a luchador hero so the game would "come from a genuine place." https://www.foxnews.com/tech/video-games-embrace-non-white-protagonists. Chicano game writer Jorge Albor still found the game's use of iconography ambivalent: https://games.ucla.edu/blog/playing-mexican-in-guacamelee
  - Remezcla's critics showed how non-Latino reviewers miss what matters (4.2).
- **[I]**
  - Hire paid Mexican and Mexican-American cultural consultants early, including an Indigenous consultant from any specific community you depict. Give them real authority to change the script and art.
  - Include a Mexican educator and a child-grief specialist.
  - Playtest with Mexican and Mexican-American kids and parents, and include at least one family that doesn't celebrate.
  - Credit consultants by name.
  - Ship with Mexican-Spanish localization.
  - Consider launching in Mexico at the same time or first.
  - If the game earns revenue, give a share to Mexican artisan communities or cultural organizations.

### 7.2 Art direction: characters, clothing, skin tones, setting **[I], based on 1.4 and 1.7**
- **Do:**
  - Contemporary everyday clothes: school uniforms, jeans, sneakers, aprons, a grandmother's *rebozo* shawl **[GK: common and not a stereotype]**.
  - Traditional dress only where the scene calls for it, such as regional dress worn at a specific Indigenous community's celebration, and drawn accurately with consultant input.
  - A real range of skin tones and features, including Indigenous and Afro-Mexican neighbors. Mexico is not one "mestizo look" (Latino Rebels, 1.7; Afro-Mexican erasure opinion: https://medium.com/@janelane_62637/afro-mexicans-exist-so-we-must-stop-referring-to-mexico-as-a-mestizo-nation-997429b8a9c4).
  - Avoid colorism, where heroes are light-skinned and vendors dark.
  - Indigenous characters as modern people with phones, jobs and opinions, not "ancient" figures.
  - A believable town: plaza, *kiosko* bandstand, church, *mercado*, *panadería*, *papelería*, homes with family photos, cars, Wi-Fi, a school.
  - Natural daylight and real color, not a yellow filter.
- **Don't:**
  - Sombrero-and-serape shorthand, bandoliers, giant mustaches, sleeping figures under cacti, tumbleweed deserts.
  - Luchador cameos with no reason.
  - Copied Indigenous textile patterns. Commission original designs or license and credit them (1.3).
- **Catrina and calavera art:** follow Posada's satirical elegance (1.2).

### 7.3 Music and sound **[I]**
- **Don't** use the Jarabe Tapatío ("Mexican Hat Dance") or generic mariachi stings as a "Mexico" cue (1.4).
- **Do** commission Mexican musicians and match the music to the region and the moment. Songs that come up in Mexican coverage of the season include "La Llorona," a traditional song from the Isthmus of Tehuantepec, Oaxaca (https://www.yucatan.com.mx/imagen/2022/10/30/la-musica-en-la-conmemoracion-del-dia-de-muertos-357776.html), Lila Downs' "Son de difuntos," La Santa Cecilia's "Calaverita" and *Coco*'s "Recuérdame" (https://www.aztecajalisco.com/arte-y-cultura/canciones-sobre-dia-de-muertos/). Commercial recordings need licensing.
- **[GK] Regional options to confirm with a consultant:** Purépecha *pirekua* songs (Michoacán), Huasteco *son* for Xantolo, and brass bands for Oaxacan *comparsas*.
- Use quiet, warm soundscapes at the cemetery: murmurs, guitar, candles crackling, distant church bells. No horror stingers.

### 7.4 Alcohol and tobacco on the ofrenda
- **What the sources say.** Adult ofrendas commonly include the person's favorite drink. That may be tequila, mezcal or pulque, but it can just as well be cola or *agua de jamaica*. Children get juice or milk: https://matadornetwork.com/es/bebidas-tipicas-dia-de-muertos/ . Mexico City's government lists cigarettes, tequila and mezcal among "everything the deceased liked": https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/day-of-the-dead-ofrendas/ . Indigenous authorities in Michoacán ask *visitors* not to drink at the celebrations (1.4).
- **[I] Recommendation for ages 8–13.**
  - Center Abuelo's favorites on kid-safe items, which are fully authentic: his *café de olla*, hot chocolate, a soda, *agua de jamaica*, his tamales, his newspaper, his guitar.
  - If you want to be honest that many families add a grown-up drink or cigarettes, show it only as background detail or one line of dialogue ("Some families put out whatever the person loved, even grown-up drinks"). Don't make these interactive, collectible or scored.
  - No drunk characters and no tequila jokes anywhere.
  - **[GK]** This also keeps you clear of rating descriptors such as ESRB "Alcohol Reference" and "Tobacco Reference." Check with your rating board.

### 7.5 Scoring or grading the ofrenda
- **What the sources say.** There is "no wrong or right way," altars differ by region and family, and even the meanings of elements vary (Section 5).
- **[I]** Telling a player their ofrenda is "wrong" contradicts what the community says and could hurt Mexican or Mexican-American players whose family does it differently. Instead:
  - Use no stars, points or "perfect ofrenda" achievements for the altar's layout.
  - Let the player choose two, three or seven tiers, or a simple table. Accept any arrangement.
  - Frame what's needed as *story* goals, not rules: "Abuela wants Abuelo's photo and his favorite pan dulce on it." Present it as her family's way, not *the* way.
  - Give feedback that rewards personal meaning and learning: Abuela smiles and tells a story about the item. Use optional "Did you know?" cards: "Many families say the marigolds' scent helps guide the souls home."
  - If an item doesn't belong, such as a plastic jack-o'-lantern, have Abuela kindly explain why her family doesn't use it. Don't flash "Wrong!"
  - Put any knowledge check in a separate, low-stakes quiz about *meanings and history*, not about the player's altar. Accept multiple answers where sources disagree, such as the meaning of the tiers.

### 7.6 Humor and the calaverita poem
- **What the sources say.**
  - *Calaveritas literarias* are short, humorous, satirical verses about death. They were consolidated as social and political satire in the 19th-century press and were sometimes censored. https://www.nationalgeographicla.com/historia/2025/10/la-tradicion-del-dia-de-muertos-que-se-burla-de-la-muerte-asi-nacieron-las-calaveras-literarias-mexicanas ; https://revistas.usp.br/caracol/article/view/227525 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_Calavera
  - Mexican schoolchildren commonly write them as homework, often teasing teachers or classmates (La Opinión, citing BBC Mundo): https://laopinion.com/?p=1997238
  - A primary school in Culiacán dedicated calaveritas to its teachers: https://www.tusbuenasnoticias.com/amp/noticias/culiacan/2023/11/24/26225-calaveritas-literarias-por-ninos-y-ninas-de-la-escuela-primaria-maria-ruben-aguirre-ulloa-en-culiacan
  - The SEP's 2026 National Calaverita Contest is open to public-school students from 5th–6th grade of primary school upward. It asks for 4–6 four-line stanzas about figures from Mexican history, "con un toque de humor, imaginación y sátira" (with a touch of humor, imagination and satire): https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/concurso-calaveritas-sep ; https://www.chilango.com/noticias/calaveritas-literarias-sep-concurso-nacional-estudiantes-fechas-clave/
  - Spanish-language how-to guides advise writing them with affection and humor, thinking of the person they're for: https://www.lifeder.com/calaveritas-literarias/ ; https://www.tusbuenasnoticias.com/noticias/mexico/2023/09/14/23683-calaveritas-literarias-chistosas-para-maestros
  - US curriculum units: https://teachers.yale.edu/curriculum/units/2024/3/6 ; https://madridcomunica.com/?p=30588
- **[I] Tone rules for the game.**
  - *Affectionate teasing*: punch up or sideways, never down. Good targets are habits and quirks: the baker who always burns the first batch, the vendor who haggles forever, a teacher's endless homework, Abuela's legendary chancla.
  - Not appearance, weight, disability, ethnicity, accent, poverty or a real recent loss. Never mock the grieving.
  - Use the classic structure: La Muerte or La Flaca comes for someone, and they outwit her, bargain with her or tag along with her in a silly way.
  - Offer a template (four-line rhyming stanzas, **[GK]** traditionally eight-syllable lines) and word banks. Filter free text for insults.
  - Let players write about a classmate, a family member, a historical figure (as the SEP does) or one of the game's characters. Don't steer them to write a joke poem about the deceased grandfather.
  - Elsewhere, humor can be gentle and character-driven, such as *Coco*'s comic skeletons. Avoid gags with real remains or graves.

### 7.7 Pan de muerto, papel picado and sugar skull activities
- **Pan de muerto.** The dough "bones" (*canillas*) and the central ball are often read as bones and a skull. Some read them as tears or the four directions. Milenio notes that meanings and forms vary by community: https://amp.milenio.com/cultura/significado-huesitos-pan-muerto . Oaxaca's *pan de yema* carries little painted faces: https://www.yucatan.com.mx/imagen/2025/10/27/tipos-de-pan-de-muerto-en-mexico-conoce-las-diferentes-variaciones-regionales.html . **[I]** Offer regional variants and accept any shaping. Have the baker share "some say…" meanings.
- **Papel picado.** San Salvador Huixcolotla, Puebla, is called its birthplace. Artisans stack sheets of *papel de China* (tissue paper) and cut them with chisels, a craft passed down through generations. Hand-made Huixcolotla papel picado was declared Puebla state heritage in 1998. https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/papel-picado-huixcolotla.html ; https://www.milenio.com/cultura/pueblo-de-papel-picado ; https://www.sdpnoticias.com/estilo-de-vida/patrimonio-cultural-picado-papel-san.html . **[I]** Model the minigame on chisel-cutting a stack, not scissors-folding. Name Huixcolotla and its artisans in-game.
- **Sugar skulls.** *Alfeñique* is a sugar-paste technique of Arabic origin that reached Mexico through Spain. Toluca's Feria del Alfeñique showcases families who have made them for generations, and the craft is recognized as State of Mexico heritage. https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/calaveras-de-azucar.html ; https://cdmxsecreta.com/feria-del-alfenique-toluca-2025/ ; https://www.tusbuenasnoticias.com/amp/noticias/mexico/2025/10/28/52386-dona-trini-es-de-la-cuarta-generacion-familiar-haciendo-calaveritas-de-dulce-en-edomex . **[GK]** Names are often written on the forehead. **[I]** Let the player write Abuelo's name, or any name, on the skull. Make decorating free-form and unscored.

### 7.8 The cemetery vigil
- **What the sources say.**
  - Families clean and decorate graves (Learning for Justice, NPR).
  - Mixquic's *Alumbrada* lights thousands of candles, and local families invite visitors into their homes (Chilango, 1.7).
  - Michoacán's Indigenous council asks visitors not to drink, not to step on altars or tombs, and to respect the rituals (Excélsior, 1.4).
  - Janitzio closed its cemetery to tourists on 1–2 November 2026 for repairs, leaving it to families: https://www.cronica.com.mx/estados/2026/10/22/cierran-panteon-de-janitzio-a-turistas-durante-dia-de-muertos-por-obras-de-rehabilitacion/
  - Respectful-visitor guides: observe quietly, ask before photographing, don't point cameras at mourners or during prayers, don't touch offerings, accept food if offered but don't ask for a photo op: https://oaxacaculture.com/tag/respect ; https://www.mexicotravelandleisure.com/blog/pomuch-day-of-the-dead/
  - EWA describes New Mexico teachers who walked young students to a cemetery to pay respects: https://ewa.org/latino-ed-beat/how-dia-de-los-muertos-fits-in-the-classroom
- **[I] Do:**
  - A warm, candlelit, crowded, social night: families chatting, sharing tamales and *atole*, someone playing guitar, kids dozing on blankets.
  - Let the player join their own family's tasks: sweep, wash the headstone, lay marigold petals, light candles, sit and listen to stories.
  - Include an NPC who models good manners, for example asking a family before taking a photo, and NPCs who offer food.
- **[I] Don't:**
  - Horror cues: fog, crows, creaking gates, glowing eyes.
  - Clickable "loot" or hidden-object hunts on other families' graves.
  - Interactions that interrupt praying or weeping NPCs. Mark those NPCs as "quiet" and let the player just nod.
  - Exposed remains.

### 7.9 The Xoloitzcuintle and other animals
- **[I]** Make the Xolo a beloved family dog, cared for, not a stray (see La Izquierda Diario's critique of *Coco*, 4.2). Let Abuela *tell* the Mexica belief about Xolos guiding souls to Mictlán as a story ("The old Mexica believed…"), sourced to 4.1. Don't make it the game's literal metaphysics.
- **[I]** Monarch butterflies make a gentle, sourced symbol for visiting souls (4.1).
- **[I]** If pets can be honored, say pet ofrendas are a newer, popular custom (1.5). Don't present them as ancient tradition.

### 7.10 Encouraging family conversations and supporting Mexican artisans
- **What the sources say.**
  - Educators use letters to caretakers and family contributions (TeachersFirst, Comprehensible Classroom, 2.2).
  - Colorín Colorado lists bilingual Día de Muertos books for families, such as *The Ofrenda That We Built*, a counting book, and a papel picado lift-the-flap book: https://www.colorincolorado.org/topics-young-children/holidays-and-celebrations?page=2 ; https://www.colorincolorado.org/themes-children/cultural-traditions?page=1
  - Guides for outsiders say to attend community-run events and buy from Latino-owned vendors (2.3).
  - Mexico's Culture Ministry pushes brands to work directly with artisan communities (1.3).
- **[I]**
  - End the game with an *invitation*, not homework: "Every family remembers in its own way. If you'd like, ask a grown-up in your family about someone they remember." Add an alternative for kids who'd rather not, or whose families don't celebrate.
  - Add a "Learn more" screen linking to Mexican and Mexican-American institutions: INAH, the National Museum of Mexican Art, Mexic-Arte Museum's educator guide (https://mexic-artemuseum.org/mexicarte/images\uploads\education\Day_of_Dead_Guide.pdf), the Smithsonian NMAI.
  - Name real craft towns such as Huixcolotla and Toluca.
  - If you sell merchandise, license it from or partner with Mexican artisans. Consider donating a share to those communities.

## 8. Design-rule checklist

**Framing and content**
- [ ] Frame it as love, memory, family and celebration of life, while letting grief be real.
- [ ] No Halloween imagery, horror cues, jump scares or "spooky" palette in Día de Muertos content.
- [ ] Spirits are beloved, specific people shown through gentle signs (petals, candles, monarchs, the Xolo). Never monsters, enemies, zombies or loot. Skeletons follow *Coco*: warm eyes, painted faces, personality.
- [ ] Describe beliefs rather than prescribe them ("many families believe…", "in our town…"). Present Catholic and Indigenous layers together, with no ranking and no single "true" version.
- [ ] Credit Indigenous roots by naming specific, living peoples. Say that historians debate the mix of Indigenous and Spanish-Catholic origins. No "3,000-year-old Aztec festival" claims.
- [ ] Root the fictional town in one region and mention that other regions and families differ. Don't imply every Mexican celebrates.
- [ ] Don't invent "traditions." Label recent customs, such as pet ofrendas, as recent. Keep out alebrijes-as-tradition, piñatas, and mixed-up Maya and Mexica myth.

**Ofrenda activity**
- [ ] Abuelo is the built-in honoree. Any "make your own" mode is optional and offers pets, historical figures, admired people, ancestors or fictional characters. Never require a real loss.
- [ ] No photo uploads of real people. Include a gentle "it's OK to feel sad" message.
- [ ] Don't grade the altar. Allow any tier count or layout. Give story goals instead of rules, feedback based on personal meaning, and a kind explanation (never "Wrong!") for items that don't belong.
- [ ] Religious items are optional and unscored. The player never has to pray.

**Representation and art**
- [ ] No sombrero, serape or poncho shorthand, no giant mustaches, no sleeping figures, no bandidos, no cactus-and-tumbleweed sets, no yellow filter.
- [ ] Modern everyday clothes and town life. Traditional dress only in accurate, consultant-checked context.
- [ ] A range of skin tones, including Indigenous and Afro-Mexican characters as modern people, with no colorism.
- [ ] Catrina and Catrín are elegant satire. Never sexualized, gory or a Halloween costume.
- [ ] Face paint, if included, appears only within a Mexican community celebration, is explained, and is optional and cosmetic.
- [ ] No copied Indigenous textile designs, and no trademarking of the holiday or its symbols.

**Sound, alcohol, humor, cemetery**
- [ ] No "Mexican Hat Dance" or mariachi stings as a "Mexico" cue. Commission Mexican musicians and use music that fits the region.
- [ ] No drunk or tequila jokes. Abuelo's favorites are kid-safe. Grown-up drinks and cigarettes appear at most as non-interactive background or one honest line.
- [ ] Calaveritas use affectionate teasing about quirks, never appearance, identity or grief. Provide templates and word banks, filter free text, and don't target the deceased grandfather.
- [ ] The cemetery is warm and social. The player tends their own family's grave. No looting or hunting on others' graves, no interrupting mourners, and an NPC models asking before taking photos.

**Language**
- [ ] Characters say "Día de Muertos." The English UI says "Day of the Dead (Día de Muertos)." Accents always (Día, cempasúchil, Mictlán).
- [ ] Mexican Spanish written and voiced by Mexicans, with a full Mexican-Spanish version. No token Spanish sprinkling. Include a glossary with pronunciation checked by a native speaker.

**Process and giving back**
- [ ] Paid Mexican, Mexican-American and (where relevant) Indigenous consultants from the start, with real authority. Add a child-grief advisor and a Mexican educator. Credit them all.
- [ ] Playtest with Mexican and Mexican-American kids and parents, plus families who don't celebrate.
- [ ] End with an optional invitation to talk with family, a "Learn more" screen pointing to Mexican institutions, named artisan towns (Huixcolotla, Toluca), and any merchandise made with Mexican artisans, with a share of revenue going back.

## 9. Open questions for your cultural consultants **[I]**
1. Which region and which community is the fictional town based on? That choice drives the dates, foods, music, altar style, and whether the cemetery vigil happens on the 1st or the 2nd.
2. Should spirits ever be visible, or only implied? How literal should the Xolo-to-Mictlán story be?
3. Should Abuela's ofrenda show any adult drink or cigarettes as background, or leave them out entirely?
4. Should the game mention *angelitos*, the children who have died, and how?
5. Which religious items belong on this family's ofrenda?
6. Is there a living artisan community you can partner with for papel picado, alfeñique or pan de muerto content, and credit or pay?
7. Should the story include the modern *pedir calaverita* custom and the Halloween crossover, or keep the focus elsewhere?
