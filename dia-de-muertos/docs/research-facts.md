## 0. Common myths and oversimplifications to avoid

1. **"It's Mexican Halloween."** It is not. The origins, meaning and tone are different. The main historical overlap is the Catholic calendar: Halloween is the eve of All Saints' Day, Nov 1. Today the two holidays mix in Mexico, for example kids in costumes asking for *calaverita* (see 1.6).
2. **"It's a spooky, sad, or death-worshipping holiday."** It is a time to remember and welcome loved ones, with both grief and joy. The returning souls are honored guests, not scary ghosts (see 1.2).
3. **"It's 100% Aztec" or "It's 100% Spanish/Catholic."** It is a blend, and scholars debate how much comes from each side. The flower cempasúchil, copal and the idea of feeding visiting ancestors have Indigenous roots. The Nov 1–2 dates, wheat bread, wax candles, sugar figures and La Catrina are European, colonial or modern (see 3.8–3.11).
4. **"All Mexicans celebrate it the same way."** There is huge regional and Indigenous variety, and some families, for example many evangelical Protestants, don't celebrate at all (see 1.3 and Section 8).
5. **"La Catrina is an Aztec goddess" or "she is Mictecacíhuatl."** La Catrina is a 20th-century satirical image. José Guadalupe Posada drew it around 1910–1913, and Diego Rivera named it "La Catrina" in his 1946–47 mural. A link to Mictecacíhuatl is a modern interpretation (see 6.2–6.3).
6. **"Alebrijes are ancient spirit guides."** Pedro Linares invented alebrijes in Mexico City in 1936. Pixar's *Coco* (2017) popularized the idea that they are spirit guides (see 6.8).
7. **"An ofrenda must have 7 levels, and each level has a fixed meaning."** Many ofrendas are a single table. The meanings given for the levels are modern and inconsistent (see 4.19).
8. **"Each papel picado color has a traditional meaning."** The color lists online contradict each other, and no traditional code is documented (see 6.7).
9. **"Italian missionaries brought sugar skulls in the 1600s."** No scholarly source was found for this. The documented root is the Arab-Spanish sugar-paste technique *alfeñique*, which became Mexican colonial confectionery (see 5.4).
10. **"Pan de muerto replaced a bread made from human-sacrifice hearts."** This is a popular legend with no documentation (see 5.2).
11. **"A Xoloitzcuintle (and only a Xolo) carried every soul across the river to Mictlán."** The 16th-century friar Sahagún describes a reddish (*bermejo*) dog, and says white and black dogs refused to help. The Xolo's link to death is real, but the popular story is simplified (see 3.5 and 6.10).
12. **"Mictlán has exactly nine named levels, as all Aztec sources agree."** The tidy nine-level list comes mainly from one colonial manuscript, and the sources differ (see 3.4).
13. **"Mexico City's Day of the Dead parade is an ancient tradition."** It started in 2016, and was at least partly inspired by the James Bond film *Spectre* (2015) (see 8.6).
14. **"October 27, the day for pets, is traditional."** It is a recent custom, popularized around 2019 (see 2.4).
15. **"The Spanish name is always 'Día de los Muertos'."** Mexico mostly says **Día de Muertos**. "Día de los Muertos" is common in the US (see 1.4).
16. **"UNESCO listed [a particular town]'s celebration."** The UNESCO element covers the Indigenous festivity in Mexico as a whole. Claims that a single town, such as Mixquic or Huaquechula, is UNESCO-listed are inaccurate (see 1.7).
17. **"*Coco*'s rules are traditional beliefs."** The film invented the "Final Death" when no one remembers you, the photo needed to cross a border, and the marigold bridge. Real petal paths and the importance of remembering inspired them, but they are not traditional beliefs (BK; see 9.9).
18. **"Santa Muerte is part of Día de Muertos."** Santa Muerte is a separate and controversial folk devotion. Don't merge the two (BK).
19. **"All Mexicans believe monarch butterflies are souls."** Government sources record this belief among specific Indigenous peoples, mainly Purépecha, Mazahua and Matlatzinca. It has since spread as popular lore (see 6.9).
20. **"'La muerte es democrática' is a verified Posada quote."** No primary source for it was found. Posada was an engraver, and other people wrote the texts on his broadsides (see 6.4).
21. **"Painting your face as a sugar skull is an old ritual."** The Catrina face paint and costumes are a modern festival and parade custom (BK; see 6.12).

## 1. Basics

**1.1 What the holiday is.** Día de Muertos is a Mexican tradition, with Indigenous and Catholic roots, in which families remember their deceased loved ones. They welcome back the souls, which are believed to return for a short visit around Nov 1–2. Families build *ofrendas* (offerings or home altars), clean and decorate graves, and share food.
*Sources:* https://www.britannica.com/topic/Day-of-the-Dead ; https://americanindian.si.edu/online-resources/topic-listing?topic=3 ; https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/00054 . **[solid]**

**1.2 Meaning and tone.** It celebrates the lives of the dead and the bond with them. Joy, memory, humor (see calaveras in Section 6) and real grief all mix together. A Mexican university essay contrasts it with the original Halloween: in Mexican tradition, death is seen as a passage, not as punishment and fear. For families who lost someone recently, the first Day of the Dead can be very emotional (see 2.6).
*Sources:* https://puedjs.unam.mx/goooya/dos-formas-opuestas-de-ver-la-muerte/ ; https://communityaltar.wisc.edu/2020/10/21/top-10-things-to-know-about-day-of-the-dead/ (summarizes National Geographic's "Top 10 things to know about the Day of the Dead"). **[solid]**

**1.3 Who celebrates.**
- It is celebrated in Mexico and by Mexican and Mexican-American communities abroad, especially in the US. Britannica says it is observed "to a lesser extent elsewhere".
- Not every Mexican family celebrates. Many evangelical or Protestant Mexicans don't build altars or bring food to graves. In cities the holiday is becoming more secular, while in much of rural Mexico it is still a vital religious practice. Christianity Today reports the custom is more common among people from a Catholic background.
- Practices range from huge altars to a single photo and candle.

*Sources:* https://www.britannica.com/topic/Day-of-the-Dead ; https://www.christianitytoday.com/2021/10/day-of-dead-christians-participate-dia-de-los-muertos/ ; https://www.christianitytoday.com/2023/10/death-latinos-dia-de-muertos-difuntos-finados-santos/ ; https://newsinfo.inquirer.net/300866/as-mexicans-mark-day-of-the-dead-some-no-longer-believe . **[solid]** that participation is not universal. **[varies by region/family]** in how people take part.

**1.4 "Día de Muertos" vs "Día de los Muertos".**
- Mexican government and cultural institutions consistently title their pages **"Día de Muertos"** (Secretaría de Cultura, INAFED, INPI, INAH).
- **"Día de los Muertos"** is the common form in the US and in English-language media, including Smithsonian event names. Britannica lists both forms. Both are understood, and neither is "wrong".
- Recommendation for the game: in a Mexico-set game, use "Día de Muertos" as the main name and mention "Día de los Muertos" as the common US variant. You may also hear *Días de Muertos*, *Todos Santos* or *Fieles Difuntos*, plus regional names (see 1.5).
- Note: the claim that Mexicans mostly drop the "los" appears mainly in travel and language blogs. The gob.mx page titles are the stronger evidence.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/cultura/prensa/dia-de-muertos-una-de-las-celebraciones-fundamentales-de-la-cultura-mexicana ; https://www.gob.mx/inafed/articulos/dia-de-muertos-tradicion-mexicana-que-trasciende-en-el-tiempo ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Day-of-the-Dead ; https://latino.si.edu/about/press-room/smithsonian-celebrates-day-dead . **[solid]**

**1.5 Regional and Indigenous names.** Several communities have their own names for the celebration:
- **Hanal Pixán** (Yucatec Maya, "food of the souls"; see 8.3).
- **Xantolo** (Huasteca; see 8.5).
- **Animecha Kejtzitakua** (Purépecha, "offering for the souls"; see 7.4).
- **Xandu'** (Isthmus Zapotec; see 8.2).

*Sources:* in Section 8. **[solid]**

**1.6 Relationship to Halloween.**
- Halloween ("All Hallows' Eve", Oct 31) is the eve of All Saints' Day. It is usually traced to the Celtic festival Samhain, and in its modern form it is about costumes, trick-or-treating and fright.
- The Smithsonian says the holidays' "only cultural connection" is their link to the Christian All Saints' Day on Nov 1.
- Today Halloween is popular in Mexico too. On Oct 31 kids may wear costumes while asking for *calaverita* (see 7.5).
- A minority academic view traces both holidays back to the same Christian season and to Samhain.

*Sources:* https://www.si.edu/newsdesk/releases/smithsonian-celebrates-day-dead ; https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/tendencias/dia-de-muertos-vs-halloween-estas-son-las-diferencias-entre-ambas-celebraciones/ ; https://puedjs.unam.mx/goooya/dos-formas-opuestas-de-ver-la-muerte/ ; https://catedra-laicidad.juridicas.unam.mx/sites/default/files/DiademuertosenMexico.pdf . **[solid]** that they are distinct holidays. **[contested]** only on the deep-history link.

**1.7 UNESCO recognition (verified).**
- The element is named **"Indigenous festivity dedicated to the dead"**. The Spanish UNESCO page renders it as *las fiestas indígenas dedicadas a los muertos*.
- It was **proclaimed in 2003** as a "Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity". It was **inscribed in 2008** on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, at the committee's third session (3.COM).
- UNESCO's description says:
  - It is practised by Mexico's Indigenous communities.
  - It commemorates the temporary return of deceased relatives "at the end of October to the beginning of November".
  - It is a fusion of pre-Hispanic rites and the Catholic feasts brought by Europeans in the 16th century.
  - The dead are believed to bring prosperity or misfortune depending on how well the rituals are carried out.
- (BK) The UNESCO text also ties the season to the end of the maize harvest.
- The listing covers Mexico's tradition as a whole, not individual towns.

*Sources:* https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/00054 ; https://ich.unesco.org/es/RL/las-festividades-indigenas-dedicadas-a-los-muertos-00054 ; INAH journal *Diario de Campo* https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/diariodecampo/article/download/5599/6444/10589 ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Day-of-the-Dead . **[solid]**

## 2. Dates and schedule

**2.1 The core days.**
- In the Catholic calendar, **Nov 1** is *Todos los Santos* (All Saints) and **Nov 2** is *Fieles Difuntos* (All Souls).
- In popular Mexican practice, **Nov 1 is for children who died** (*angelitos*, "muertos chiquitos") and **Nov 2 is for adults** ("muertos grandes").
- Some communities differ. Among the Yaqui, for example, Nov 1 is for the *angelitos* and Nov 2 for the *fieles difuntos*, and among the Otomí the celebration starts on Oct 31.

*Sources:* Secretaría de Cultura booklet "Así nació el Día de Muertos" https://www.cultura.gob.mx/turismocultural/publi/Cuadernos_19_num/cuaderno16.pdf ; https://www.gob.mx/inafed/articulos/dia-de-muertos-tradicion-mexicana-que-trasciende-en-el-tiempo ; https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/altares-indigenas-de-dia-de-muertos-cual-quieres-para-recibir-a-los-tuyos . **[solid]**, with **[varies by region/family]** details.

**2.2 When the souls arrive and leave.** There is no single national timetable. A common pattern:

| Who | Arrive | Honored |
|---|---|---|
| Children's souls | Oct 31 | Nov 1 |
| Adults' souls | Nov 1 | Nov 2 |

- The souls are sent off on **Nov 2**. A government source says that "in many areas" they are bid farewell with a meal and fireworks (*cohetes*).
- Mixquic (Mexico City): children's souls are received from Oct 31 and adults' from Nov 1.
- INPI (one text): children's souls "arrive on Nov 1" to take in the essence and aroma of the food.
- Yucatán (Hanal Pixán): children on Oct 31, adults on Nov 1, all souls on Nov 2.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inafed/articulos/dia-de-muertos-tradicion-mexicana-que-trasciende-en-el-tiempo ; https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; https://www.merida.gob.mx/animas/tradiciones.php ; https://www.infobae.com/mexico/2024/10/31/dia-de-muertos-2024-quienes-son-las-almas-que-visitan-a-los-vivos-hoy-31-de-octubre/ . **[varies by region/family]**

**2.3 Oct 28 and the "day-by-day calendar".**
- Mexican media widely repeat a calendar in which **Oct 28** is for people who died suddenly or violently, for example in accidents. Some families light a white candle for them.
- Huaquechula (Puebla) is a concrete example: there, altars for accident victims go up on Oct 28.
- Some lists add more days: Oct 29 for the drowned, Oct 30 for "lonely" souls with no one to remember them, and Oct 31 for unbaptized children (*limbo*) or for *angelitos*. These lists disagree with each other.
- Treat this calendar as a popular custom in some places, not a universal rule.

*Sources:* https://www.yucatan.com.mx/general/2026/10/28/dia-de-muertos-2026-cuando-llegan-las-almas-que-murieron-tragicamente-y-como-recibirlas.html ; https://thehappening.com/dia-de-muertos-2026-a-quien-se-recuerda-cada-dia-del-27-de-octubre-al-2-de-noviembre/ ; https://www.eluniversalpuebla.com.mx/que-hacer/dia-de-muertos-puebla-altares-monumentales-de-huaquechula-una-tradicion-muy-poblana/ . **[varies by region/family]**

**2.4 Oct 27: pets (a modern addition).**
- Pets became associated with Oct 27 only in recent years. Infobae says there is no official source or historical record that fixes its origin.
- Several outlets say a pet funeral company ("Funeral Pet") proposed the date in 2019. This is unconfirmed by any independent source.
- Pet ofrendas include photos, toys, food and cempasúchil. Fine to include in a game if labeled as a new custom.

*Sources:* https://www.infobae.com/mexico/2026/10/27/27-de-octubre-fecha-en-que-las-mascotas-que-fallecieron-regresan-a-casa-segun-la-tradicion-popular-mexicana/ ; https://www.informador.mx/amp/estilo/por-que-hoy-27-de-octubre-regresan-las-mascotas-conoce-la-tradicion-20261027-0120.html ; https://www.milenio.com/estilo/tu-mascota/ofrenda-para-mascotas-por-que-se-celebra-hoy-27-octubre-en-mexico . **[contested or myth: it is real now, but not traditional; dates from about 2019]**

**2.5 New in 2025–26: a Xoloitzcuintle day.**
- On Apr 15, 2026, Mexico's Chamber of Deputies approved declaring **Oct 27 the "Día Nacional del Xoloitzcuintle"** (433 votes in favor). Senate ratification was not confirmed in the sources found.
- Mexico City separately declared the knowledge and practices around the Xolo to be intangible cultural heritage, effective Oct 28, 2026.

*Sources:* https://www.elimparcial.com/locurioso/2026/04/16/diputados-aprueban-el-dia-nacional-del-xoloitzcuintle-asi-se-celebrara-en-mexico/ ; https://gaceta.diputados.gob.mx/Gaceta/66/2025/dic/20251209-II-5.html ; https://www.cronica.com.mx/metropoli/2026/10/27/declaran-patrimonio-cultural-inmaterial-los-saberes-asociados-al-xoloitzcuintle-en-cdmx/ . **[solid]** as news. The national decree's final status is unverified.

**2.6 After Nov 2, and "first-year" ofrendas.**
- Some regions repeat offerings later:
  - Yucatán: the **bix** or *ochavario*, eight days later, a farewell to the souls.
  - The Huasteca: **octavas** and **veintenas**, smaller ofrendas a week and 20 days later.
- For someone who died within the past year, families often make a special ofrenda:
  - Huaquechula's monumental altars honor people who died in the roughly 11 months before.
  - Among the Isthmus Zapotec, a first celebration is called *biyé'* or *xandu' yaa*.

*Sources:* https://www.poresto.com/yucatan/2025/11/3/que-es-el-bix-tradicion-maya-despues-del-hanal-pixan-en-yucatan.html ; https://www.milenio.com/cultura/xantolo-la-fiesta-huasteca-de-todos-los-santos ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/huaquechula-la-magnificencia-de-la-muerte.html ; https://archivo.eluniversal.com.mx/estados/2014/impreso/la-fiesta-de-los-muertos-96673.html . **[varies by region/family]**

## 3. Origins

**3.1 Mesoamerican view of the afterlife.** For the Mexica (Aztecs), where you went after death depended mainly on **how you died, not on how good or bad you had been**. Most people went to Mictlán. Warriors, women who died in childbirth and the drowned went to other places (see 3.7). Britannica traces the holiday's roots to Mesoamerican peoples (Aztec, Toltec, Maya and others) who saw death as a continuation of existence in another realm.
*Sources:* https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/los-lugares-de-destino-del-teyolia ; https://www.infobae.com/america/mexico/2022/11/01/del-tlalocan-al-mictlan-cuales-son-los-cielos-a-los-que-los-muertos-viajan-segun-las-creencias-de-la-epoca-prehispanica/ ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Day-of-the-Dead . **[solid]**

**3.2 Mexica festivals of the dead: Miccailhuitontli and Hueymiccailhuitl.**
- The Mexica honored the dead in two consecutive 20-day "months" (*veintenas*) of their 365-day calendar (*xiuhpōhualli*):
  - **Tlaxochimaco**, also called **Miccailhuitontli**, "the little feast of the dead".
  - **Xócotl Huetzi** ("the fruit falls"), also called **Hueymiccailhuitl**, "the great feast of the dead".
- By common calendar correlations they fell around **(July–)August**. The exact correlation is debated.
- The friar Diego Durán (16th century) read the "little" feast as one for dead children ("fiesta de los muertecitos") and the great feast as one for adults. Scholars note this may be Durán's own interpretation.
- In both, food and objects were offered to the dead.
- Spelling variants: Miccaílhuitontli; Huey Miccaílhuitl.

*Sources:* https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/miccailhuitontli-y-huey-miccailhuitl ; https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/miccailhuitontli/188910 ; http://www.hemi.nyu.edu/cuaderno/diademuertos/sitio/introduccion/menu.html ; https://www.gaceta.unam.mx/miccailhuitontli-fiesta-mesoamericana-origen-del-pan-de-muerto/ ; Secretaría de Cultura booklet (link in 2.1). **[solid]** that the festivals existed. **[contested]** on the children/adults split and the exact dates.

**3.3 Mictlán.** The Mexica "place of the dead" (Nahuatl *Mictlān*, written *Mictlán* in Spanish). It was the destination of people who died of ordinary or natural causes. It was ruled by **Mictlantecuhtli** and **Mictecacíhuatl**. It was **not a place of punishment** like the Christian hell.
*Sources:* https://www.cronica.com.mx/tendencias/2026/10/18/dia-de-muertos-2026-en-cdmx-descubre-los-cuatro-destinos-de-la-muerte-mexica/ ; https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/ask-us/13-heavens-and-9-underworlds . **[solid]**

**3.4 The journey and the "nine levels".**
- The journey to Mictlán was described as long and hard, commonly said to take **four years**, through a series of trials.
- (BK) The Florentine Codex (Sahagún, Book III) lists these trials: hills that crash together, a serpent, a lizard, eight deserts and eight hills, and a wind of obsidian blades. Then came a river, and finally the soul reached Mictlantecuhtli.
- The neat list of **nine named levels** in modern retellings comes mainly from **one colonial manuscript, the Codex Vaticanus A (Ríos)**. Other sources don't match it or each other, and the Florentine Codex names some stages differently. Some scholars think the stacked-levels model shows European influence.
- For the game: fine to present the nine levels as "one version of the story", not as fixed fact.

*Sources:* https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/underworld/the-sinister-road-the-nine-levels-of-mictlan-10 ; https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/ask-us/13-heavens-and-9-underworlds ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mictl%C4%81n (a weaker source, cited for the four-year figure). **[contested or myth: the details vary between sources, and the nine levels are mainly one manuscript's list]**

**3.5 The river and the dog.**
- Sahagún wrote that the dead crossed a river, **Chiconahuapan** (variants include Chicunahuapa and Apanohuayan), riding on a dog.
- The dog was a **reddish (*bermejo*) dog**. **White and black dogs refused**: white dogs said they were clean and didn't want to get dirty, and black dogs said they were dirty and would dirty the river.
- Dogs were sometimes buried with the dead to act as guides, a custom that continued after the Conquest.
- Modern popular versions specify a Xoloitzcuintle and add moral conditions, such as "only if you were kind to dogs". Those conditions are not in Sahagún's account as reported.

*Sources:* https://www.infobae.com/mexico/2026/10/17/el-origen-de-los-perros-guia-al-mictlan-que-razas-acompanaban-el-viaje-de-los-muertos-antes-del-xoloitzcuintle/ ; https://expansion.mx/tendencias/2025/10/20/por-que-mexicas-creian-perro-te-guia-mictlan-despues-morir ; https://www.inah.gob.mx//foto-del-dia/xoloitzcuintle-companero-en-el-viaje-al-mictlan ; https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/la-antigua-leyenda-del-xoloitzcuintle/ . **[solid]** for the core (a dog helps the soul cross). **[contested]** for "always a Xolo" and the moral conditions.

**3.6 Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacíhuatl.**
- **Mictlantecuhtli** (*Mictlāntēcuhtli*), "Lord of Mictlán", is shown skeletal or half-fleshed.
  - In 1994, archaeologists found two life-size ceramic statues of him in the House of the Eagles next to the Templo Mayor. They date to the time of Moctezuma I (1440–1469). One is about 1.76 m tall and is in the Museo del Templo Mayor.
- **Mictecacíhuatl** (*Mictēcacihuātl*), "Lady of the place of the dead", is his consort.
- Popular claims go further. They say she "presided over the ancient festivals of the dead", that she died at birth, or that La Catrina descends from her. These claims are weakly documented, and even popular sources admit there is no documented link between Posada and the goddess.
- Game note: present both as deities of death in a cycle of life, not as "devils".

*Sources:* https://repositorio.inah.gob.mx/o-35426 ; https://www.mexicolore.co.uk/aztecs/artefacts/spotlight/mictlantecuhtli ; https://www.scielo.org.mx/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S2007-249X2014000100007 ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/mictecacihuatl-diosa-de-la-muerte.html ; https://historia.nationalgeographic.com.es/a/historia-catrina-caricatura-belica-simbolo-dia-muertos_20418 . **[solid]** for Mictlantecuhtli. **[contested]** for the popular stories about Mictecacíhuatl.

**3.7 Other afterlife destinations.**
- **Tonatiuh Ichan** ("House of the Sun"; also *Tonatiuhichan* or *Ilhuícatl Tonatiuh*):
  - For warriors who died in battle or as sacrifices, women who died giving birth (honored as warriors) and merchants who died on trading expeditions.
  - (BK) Sahagún says the warriors later returned as hummingbirds and butterflies.
- **Tlalocan**, the green paradise of the rain god Tláloc:
  - For those who died from water-related causes such as drowning or lightning, or from certain diseases. The disease lists vary between sources.
- **Chichihuacuauhco** ("place of the wet-nurse tree"):
  - For babies who died while still nursing, fed by a tree that dripped milk. One version says it was for babies who died before eating maize.
- Some lists add Cincalco or Omeyocan. These lists differ by source.

*Sources:* https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/los-lugares-de-destino-del-teyolia ; https://www.infobae.com/america/mexico/2022/11/01/del-tlalocan-al-mictlan-cuales-son-los-cielos-a-los-que-los-muertos-viajan-segun-las-creencias-de-la-epoca-prehispanica/ ; https://matadornetwork.com/es/paraisos-mexica-despues-de-la-muerte/ (less rigorous). **[solid]** for the core. **[varies]** in the details.

**3.8 The Catholic side and the blend.**
- The Spanish brought **All Saints' Day (Nov 1)** and **All Souls' Day (Nov 2)** in the 16th century. All Souls' Day is traced to Abbot Odilo of Cluny, in France, around 998 (BK for the date).
- After the conquest, Indigenous ways of honoring the dead blended with Catholic practices: prayers for souls in purgatory, candles, altars and bread. A Secretaría de Cultura booklet calls the result the product of two traditions, Hispanic and pre-Hispanic. UNESCO describes it as a fusion of two worldviews.

*Sources:* https://www.britannica.com/topic/Day-of-the-Dead ; https://www.cultura.gob.mx/turismocultural/publi/Cuadernos_19_num/cuaderno16.pdf ; https://cortamortaja.mx/2021/11/01/dia-de-muertos-no-tiene-origen-prehispanico-elsa-malvido/ ; https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/00054 . **[solid]**

**3.9 How much is pre-Hispanic and how much is European? The scholarly debate, summarized fairly.**
- **The European and colonial view.**
  - Historian **Elsa Malvido** (INAH, died 2011) argued that the holiday is essentially a colonial, Catholic and mestizo tradition. In her view, altars, sugar skulls and bone-shaped breads come from medieval European Catholic customs, especially Jesuit ones, and 20th-century intellectuals gave them a pre-Hispanic "national" meaning.
  - Anthropologist **Stanley Brandes** (UC Berkeley) wrote *Skulls to the Living, Bread to the Dead* (2006). He found that pre-Conquest festivals for the dead existed, but little direct continuity for most specific customs, which match Catholic or European practice.
- **The Indigenous-roots view.** UNESCO and many INAH and UNAM researchers stress Indigenous roots:
  - care for and feeding of the dead, cempasúchil, copal and dogs as guides;
  - the season's link to the maize harvest;
  - the distinct Indigenous forms: Hanal Pixán, Xantolo and Animecha Kejtzitakua.
  - A UNAM historian, Abigail Mesa (quoted by Milenio), disagrees with Malvido.
- **The history of the national symbol.** **Claudio Lomnitz** (*Death and the Idea of Mexico*, 2005) traces how death became a Mexican national "totem". He follows it through colonial religion, 19th-century satire and 20th-century state culture.
- **Kid-friendly summary:** "It grew from Indigenous traditions and Spanish Catholic customs mixing together, and it kept changing, even in the last 100 years."

*Sources:* https://anthropology.berkeley.edu/skulls-living-bread-dead ; https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/jfrr/article/view/40139/42320 ; https://cortamortaja.mx/2021/11/01/dia-de-muertos-no-tiene-origen-prehispanico-elsa-malvido/ ; https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/tintes-catolicos-salpican-el-dia-de-muertos-fragmentos-de-cultura-europea/1480138 ; https://www.milenio.com/politica/comunidad/origen-dia-muertos-lazaro-cardenas-invento-festividad ; https://read.dukeupress.edu/hahr/article-abstract/87/2/403/27498/Death-and-the-Idea-of-Mexico ; https://academic.oup.com/ahr/article-abstract/112/1/254/41778 ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/dia-de-muertos-prehispanico-o-espanol-catolico-o-pagano.html . **[contested: scholars debate the proportions]**

**3.10 20th-century promotion.** The post-revolutionary state, especially under Lázaro Cárdenas (1934–40) and the SEP (Mexico's education ministry), promoted Día de Muertos as a national tradition with "pre-Hispanic" meaning. Saying Cárdenas "invented" the holiday is an overstatement.
*Sources:* https://heraldodemexico.com.mx/cultura/2020/10/30/dia-de-muertos-investigadora-del-inah-revela-origen-iniciado-por-lazaro-cardenas-220640.html ; https://www.milenio.com/politica/comunidad/origen-dia-muertos-lazaro-cardenas-invento-festividad . **[contested]**

## 4. The ofrenda

**4.0 What it is.**
- An *ofrenda* (also *altar de muertos*) is a set of offerings that welcomes the souls home. It is placed in the house, often on a table and sometimes on tiers, and/or on the grave.
- Schools, offices and town squares also set up public ofrendas.
- The contents vary hugely.
- Britannica says the flowers' strong scent helps the dead find the altar, and food is offered because the dead are hungry after their journey.

*Sources:* https://www.britannica.com/topic/ofrenda ; https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/meaning-behind-six-objects-dia-de-los-muertos-altars-180973442/ . **[solid]**

**4.1 Photos.** A photo shows who is being honored and is often the emotional center of the altar. In some places the portrait is hidden so it can be seen only in a mirror, to show that the loved one can be "seen" but is no longer physically here (CDI). (BK) Many families avoid putting photos of living people on the ofrenda.
*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; http://www.cdi.gob.mx/difusion/diamuertos/ofrendas.html . **[solid]** for the photo. **[varies]** for the mirror and the living-photos belief.

**4.2 Cempasúchil (Mexican marigold, *Tagetes erecta*).**
- **Name:** from Nahuatl *cempōhualxōchitl*. *Cempōhualli* means "twenty" and *xōchitl* means "flower", so the name means **"twenty flower(s)"**.
  - Government pages also say "flower of twenty petals" or "many flowers". One source notes that "twenty" could mean "many". Those two readings are interpretations.
  - Spellings: *cempasúchil*, *cempasúchitl*, *cempoalxóchitl*, *zempasúchil*.
- **Native to Mexico.**
- **Why it's used:** its bright color is associated with the sun, and its strong scent is believed to guide the souls. The Mexica associated it with the sun and used it in funerary contexts.
- **Petal paths:** families scatter petal paths from the door or street to the altar, and in many places from the cemetery to the house and back, to lead the souls. One explanation is that the petals hold the sun's warmth and light the way.
- **Other uses:** medicine, a natural dye and insect control.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/semarnat/articulos/cempasuchil-o-veinte-flores?idiom=es ; https://gdn.iib.unam.mx/diccionario/cempohualxochitl/188766 ; https://dle.rae.es/cempas%C3%BAchil ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/articulos/cempasuchil-la-flor-de-los-veinte-petalos ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/es/articulos/flor-de-cempasuchil-una-guia-para-las-almas ; https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/cempasuchil-usos-medicinales-y-agricolas-de-la-flor-de-dia-de-muertos/ ; https://www.chapultepec.org.mx/en/flora-y-jardines/flor-de-cempasuchil/ . **[solid]** for the etymology ("twenty"), the native origin and the guiding belief. **[contested]** only for the "many petals" reading.

**4.3 Other flowers.**
- **Terciopelo**, also called **mano de león** or **cresta de gallo** (*Celosia*): a velvety, deep red or purple flower. It is often said to stand for mourning; other sources say it brings comfort.
- **Nube** (baby's breath): white, used especially on children's altars for purity and tenderness.
- **Alhelí** (stock): its meaning depends on its color.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/articulos/colores-y-simbolos-flores-para-el-altar-de-dia-de-muertos ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/flores-de-dia-de-muertos.html ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/flor-de-tercipelo.html ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/hidalgo/articulos/cempasuchil-y-mano-de-leon-flores-que-adornan-la-celebracion-del-dia-de-muertos?idiom=es . **[varies by region/family]**

**4.4 Candles and veladoras.**
- Light guides the souls home. (A *vela* is a candle, a *veladora* is a candle in a glass, and a *cirio* is a tall church candle.)
- In several Indigenous communities **each candle stands for one deceased person** (CDI).
- Four candles set in a cross can stand for the **four cardinal directions**, which orient the soul's path home. This is documented, for example, in Ocotepec, Morelos.
- Some manuals say purple accents mean mourning.
- INPI says Europeans introduced wax candles. (BK) Pre-Hispanic rituals used fire, such as pine (*ocote*) torches.

*Sources:* http://www.cdi.gob.mx/difusion/diamuertos/ofrendas.html ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/edomex/es/articulos/conoce-los-elementos-de-la-ofrenda-del-dia-de-muertos?idiom=es ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/dia-de-muertos-en-ocotepec-cuernavaca.html ; https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos . **[solid]** for the core. **[varies]** for counts and arrangements.

**4.5 Copal and the incense burner.**
- **Copal** (Nahuatl *copalli*) is an aromatic resin from *Bursera* trees, such as *Bursera bipinnata*. Large amounts of it have been found in the Templo Mayor offerings.
- It is burned in a clay **sahumador** (also called *sahumerio*). Its smoke purifies the space, and many say the fragrant smoke attracts or guides the souls.
- The Nahuatl name of the burner:
  - Archaeologists call the Mexica ladle-shaped censer a ***tlemaitl*** ("fire hand").
  - Classical dictionaries record *popōchtli* (incense) and *popōchcaxitl* (censer).
  - **"Popoxcomitl" / "popochcomitl"** turns up mostly in modern ritual and craft-selling contexts. No dictionary entry for it was found.
  - For the game, the Spanish *sahumador* is the safest word.

*Sources:* https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/el-copal-en-las-ofrendas-del-templo-mayor ; https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/efluvios-mensajeros-el-copal-y-el-yauhtli-en-los-sahumadores-del-templo-mayor ; https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/arqueologia/en/article/download/6278/7110 ; https://nahuatl.wired-humanities.org/content/popochcaxitl ; https://www.infobae.com/mexico/2026/10/15/muchos-lo-ponen-en-la-ofrenda-pero-pocos-saben-su-origen-el-verdadero-significado-del-copal-y-el-incienso-en-dia-de-muertos/ . **[solid]** for copal. **[contested or myth: "popoxcomitl" is a modern, weakly attested term]**

**4.6 Water.** It quenches the souls' thirst after their long journey. Some also link it to purity and life.
*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/day-of-the-dead-ofrendas/?lang=en ; https://artsandculture.google.com/story/the-meaning-of-the-altar-uslatinomuseum/_QUBc67lRPvBIQ?hl=en (Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Latino). **[solid]**

**4.7 Salt.** It stands for purification, "so the soul is not corrupted" on its journey.
*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; https://nuevaescuelamexicana.sep.gob.mx/contenido/recurso/elementos-basicos-para-la-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos/ . **[solid]** as a common explanation. **[varies]**

**4.8 Pan de muerto.** It is offered as a welcome and as a sign of affection and fellowship. See 5.1–5.3.
*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos . **[solid]**

**4.9 Sugar skulls (calaveritas de azúcar).** They are placed for the dead, often with names written on the forehead. (BK) Sugar skulls with friends' names are also given as gifts to the living, which is the meaning behind Brandes's book title *Skulls to the Living, Bread to the Dead*. See 5.4.
*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/es/articulos/calaverita-de-azucar-dulce-sabor-de-la-muerte ; https://anthropology.berkeley.edu/skulls-living-bread-dead . **[solid]**

**4.10 Papel picado.** Cut-paper banners add color and festivity and are commonly said to represent **wind or air**. One museum says their fluttering shows the souls' presence. See 6.6–6.7.
*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; https://learn.ncartmuseum.org/resources/day-of-the-dead/ . **[solid]** as a common explanation.

**4.11 Favorite foods and drinks.**
- The dead person's favorite dishes are offered. Common ones include mole, tamales, atole, fruit and sweets.
- **Children:** candy, mild (non-spicy) food, milk or atole, and toys.
- **Adults:** often their favorite drinks, including alcohol such as tequila, mezcal, pulque or beer, and cigarettes if they smoked.
- Game note: you can show "favorite drinks" accurately without featuring alcohol.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inafed/articulos/dia-de-muertos-tradicion-mexicana-que-trasciende-en-el-tiempo ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/articulos/del-campo-a-la-ofrenda-dulces-y-platillos-para-dia-de-muertos ; https://www.personal.unam.mx/Docs/Cendi/dia-de-muertos-elementos-de-la-ofrenda.pdf . **[solid]** with **[varies by region/family]**.

**4.12 Personal objects and toys.** Families add objects the person loved, such as a hat, tools or a musical instrument, and toys for the *angelitos*.
*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inafed/articulos/dia-de-muertos-tradicion-mexicana-que-trasciende-en-el-tiempo ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/meaning-behind-six-objects-dia-de-los-muertos-altars-180973442/ . **[solid]**

**4.13 Cross and religious images.** Many ofrendas include a crucifix or cross, images of saints or the Virgin of Guadalupe, and sometimes an image of the souls in purgatory. These reflect Catholic faith. (BK) Crosses may be made of flowers, palm, salt, lime or ash, and their meanings vary by place.
*Sources:* https://artsandculture.google.com/story/nuestras-historias-day-of-the-dead-national-museum-of-mexican-art/mQWRb7ZeQriSIA?hl=en (National Museum of Mexican Art: "religious statues") ; https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar_de_muertos (weaker source). **[varies by region/family]**

**4.14 Xoloitzcuintle figure.** Many families now place a clay figure or picture of a Xolo dog on the ofrenda, as a symbolic guide or protector of the souls. This is based on the old belief about dogs and Mictlán, but as a common altar item it appears to be fairly modern.
*Sources:* https://www.elimparcial.com/locurioso/2025/10/22/dia-de-muertos-cual-es-el-significado-del-perro-xoloitzcuintle-en-la-ofrenda/ ; https://www.milenio.com/espectaculos/famosos/cual-es-la-relacion-xoloizcuintle-con-la-muerte-mitologia . **[varies by region/family]**

**4.15 The arch (*arco*).**
- An arch of reed (*carrizo*), palm, sugarcane or local wood, covered with cempasúchil and other flowers. Fruit, bread or sugar figures sometimes hang from it.
- It is most often explained as the **doorway between the world of the living and the world of the dead**.
- The arch matters especially in:
  - **The Huasteca (Xantolo):** a study in Chicontepec, Veracruz, documented **27 plant species** in the arches, with symbolism tied to the maize cycle and thanks for harvests.
  - **Puebla.**
  - **Purépecha Michoacán:** a wooden frame hung with bread, fruit and sugar figures.
- One Huasteca official says some families use 12 sticks for the months and flowers in sets of seven.

*Sources:* https://revistaetnobiologia.mx/index.php/etno/article/view/118 ; https://www.uaeh.edu.mx/gaceta/3/numero33/noviembre/xantolo-huasteca.html ; https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/nahuas-de-chicontepec-veracruz-colocan-altar-de-muertos-y-recrean-el-xantolo-en-el-museo-nacional-de-antropologia ; https://cultura.michoacan.gob.mx/noticias/secum-acercara-el-simbolismo-de-la-ofrenda-purepecha-al-museo-del-estado/ ; https://emsavalles.com/NL286563/arcos-de-xantolo-cada-varita-flor-y-fruta-guarda-un-significado-ancestral . **[solid]** as a practice. **[varies]** in exact meaning.

**4.16 Petate (woven palm mat).** It gives the souls a place to rest and also serves as a tablecloth for the food (CDI). The Mexico City government says it can symbolize the burial shroud.
*Sources:* http://www.cdi.gob.mx/difusion/diamuertos/ofrendas.html ; https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/day-of-the-dead-ofrendas/?lang=en . **[varies by region/family]**

**4.17 Washbasin, soap, towel and mirror.**
- A washbasin (*aguamanil*), soap and towel let the souls wash their hands after the long journey (INPI, CDI).
- **Mirror:** in some places it hides or reflects the portrait (see 4.1). In Huaquechula, a mirror in front of the photo is said to reflect the soul.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; http://www.cdi.gob.mx/difusion/diamuertos/ofrendas.html ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/huaquechula-la-magnificencia-de-la-muerte.html . **[varies by region/family]**

**4.18 The "four elements".** A popular explanation in schools, museums and media says the ofrenda holds:
- **earth**: food and harvest,
- **wind or air**: papel picado (some say copal smoke),
- **water**: a glass of water,
- **fire**: candles.

It is a useful teaching frame, but no source documents it as an old or universal rule.
*Sources:* https://thegracemuseum.org/learn/2020-11-4-dia-de-los-muertos-symbols-and-traditions/ ; https://www.dignitymemorial.com/grief-and-sympathy/losing-a-loved-one/day-of-the-dead-altar . **[contested or myth: a modern interpretation; fine to present as "one way people explain it"]**

**4.19 Levels or tiers.** The levels are explained in many ways:

| Levels | Common explanations |
|---|---|
| 1 | a plain table, very common |
| 2 | heaven and earth |
| 3 | heaven, earth and the underworld; or earth, purgatory and heaven; or the Holy Trinity |
| 7 | the steps the soul passes to reach rest; the seven Mexica destinations; part of Mictlán; or the seven deadly sins (which Spanish Wikipedia ties to Otomí practice) |

- Huaquechula's tiers are read as earth, the boundary between heaven and earth, and heaven.
- These explanations mostly come from news features and government outreach (for example the ISSSTE, a public-sector health agency), and they contradict each other.
- The Grace Museum notes that every Mexican state sets up ofrendas its own way: some are flat, some have several levels.

*Sources:* https://www.imagenradio.com.mx/bienestar/historia-altar-muertos-que-significa-cada-niveles ; https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altar_de_muertos ; https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/tendencias/dia-de-muertos-2024-cual-es-el-significado-de-los-7-niveles-de-la-ofrenda/ ; https://thegracemuseum.org/learn/2020-11-4-dia-de-los-muertos-symbols-and-traditions/ . **[contested or myth: there is no single meaning, and many altars have no tiers]**

**4.20 What happens to the food afterwards.**
- Widely documented belief: the souls take in the **essence, aroma and steam** of the food rather than eating it physically.
- That is why many communities serve the food hot and freshly made. In Naupan (Puebla), people say the souls take the steam of tamales, mole or coffee, and bread is set over hot coffee so the steam soaks it.
- INPI: children's souls come "to nourish themselves with the essence and the smell of the food".
- After the souls leave, families commonly **eat or share** the food with relatives and neighbors, and many say it has lost its flavor or smell.
- Some families prefer not to eat it. Modern advice is to throw away cooked dishes that sat out too long.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/conoces-el-significado-de-los-elementos-de-una-ofrenda-de-dia-de-muertos ; https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/vitabrevis/es/issue/download/237/83 ; https://nahuatl.historicas.unam.mx/index.php/ecn/article/download/77889/68940/228821 (*Estudios de Cultura Náhuatl*, UNAM: "Comer (con) los difuntos") ; https://www.timeoutmexico.mx/ciudad-de-mexico/que-hacer/ofrenda-cuando-se-quita-y-que-hacer-con-la-comida ; https://www.animalgourmet.com/2023/11/01/que-hacer-con-la-comida-de-la-ofrenda-despues-del-dia-de-muertos . **[solid]** as a belief. **[varies by region/family]** in practice.

## 5. Foods

**5.1 Pan de muerto: what the shape is said to mean.**
- A round, sweet yeast bread, often scented with orange blossom (*azahar*) and topped with sugar.
- On top: strips shaped like bones (*huesos* or *canillas*), usually in a cross, and a ball in the center.
- Common explanations:
  - the ball is a skull (or the heart, or sorrow),
  - the strips are bones and/or tears,
  - the round shape is the cycle of life,
  - the orange blossom recalls the departed.
- A pastry chef quoted by El Universal says the "four cardinal directions" explanation has no support, and that bakers call the ball the *nariz* ("nose").
- These explanations are widespread folk interpretations, not a documented original design.

*Sources:* https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/tendencias/dia-de-muertos-2024-cual-es-el-origen-y-significado-del-pan-de-muerto/ ; https://www.gob.mx/issste/es/articulos/el-pan-de-muerto ; https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/pan-de-muerto-origen-historia-simbolismo/ . **[solid]** that these explanations are common. **[contested]** as an "original meaning".

**5.2 Pan de muerto: origin.**
- Wheat came with the Spanish. Two antecedents are proposed:
  - European breads for the dead or for souls (*pan de ánimas*);
  - Indigenous ritual figures made of amaranth with honey or maguey syrup (***tzoalli***), the angle UNAM anthropologists emphasize.
- The story that **heart-shaped breads dyed red with sugar replaced human sacrifice is a popular legend without documentation**.
- The bread's exact history is hard to trace. Sources even disagree about when the name and the modern shape first appear in writing.

*Sources:* https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/pan-de-muerto-origen-historia-simbolismo/ ; https://www.gaceta.unam.mx/miccailhuitontli-fiesta-mesoamericana-origen-del-pan-de-muerto/ ; https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/tendencias/como-surgio-el-pan-de-muerto-conoce-el-verdadero-origen-y-significado-de-este-pan-de-dulce-mexicano/ ; https://ciencia.unam.mx/leer/1183/pan-de-muerto-sabor-y-tradicion-en-un-alimento . **[contested]**

**5.3 Regional breads.**

| Region | Breads |

**5.4 Calaveras de azúcar (sugar skulls) and alfeñique.**
- ***Alfeñique*** is a sugar-paste technique (sugar, water, lemon, sometimes egg white) from Islamic Spain. It comes from Arabic *al-fanid*, glossed as "thinness" or "delicate". The Spanish brought it to New Spain.
  - Old recipes kneaded the paste with ***chautle***, a sticky substance from a native orchid (*Bletia campanulata*).
  - Toluca says the first published recipe appeared in the *Cocinero mexicano* in 1831.
- Historian Teresa Castelló Yturbide credited nuns with making early sugar skulls in the 17th century, using clay molds from Metepec. Wikipedia's "Calavera" article dates the first known mention of selling skeleton figures to the 1740s.
- **"Italian missionaries brought sugar skulls in the 17th century"** appears only on blogs and school pages that copy each other. No historian's source was found for it.
- The Smithsonian's Postal Museum says the sugar represents "the sweetness of life".

*Sources:* https://gardens.si.edu/learn/blog/the-smithsonian-gardens-orchid-collection-through-an-ethnobotanical-lens/ ; https://postalmuseum.si.edu/stamp-stories-day-of-the-dead ; https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/menu/conoce-el-origen-y-significado-de-las-calaveritas-de-azucar/ ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/es/articulos/calaverita-de-azucar-dulce-sabor-de-la-muerte ; https://www2.toluca.gob.mx/alfenique-en-toluca-una-dulce-tradicion-viva-desde-hace-400-anos/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calavera (weaker source). **[solid]** that the technique is Arab-Spanish and colonial. **[contested or myth: the "Italian missionaries" story is unverified, and the exact first dates vary]**

**5.5 Toluca's Feria del Alfeñique.**
- Toluca (State of Mexico) has a centuries-old tradition of sugar figures: skulls, sheep, coffins, angels and more.
- Local tradition dates it to **1630**, when a man named Francisco de la Rosa is said to have asked the Spanish crown for permission to make alfeñique. This rests on municipal accounts and oral tradition, not independent documentation.
- The fair is held under the city's arcades (*portales*) until Nov 2. Its metal stalls date from 1985.
- Toluca's alfeñique has been declared **intangible cultural heritage of the State of Mexico**.

*Sources:* https://www2.toluca.gob.mx/alfenique-en-toluca-una-dulce-tradicion-viva-desde-hace-400-anos/ ; https://www.digitalmex.mx/municipios/story/30820/historia-feria-alfenique-toluca ; https://www.eluniversaledomex.com.mx/gobierno/historico-el-alfenique-de-toluca-es-declarado-patrimonio-cultural-inmaterial-del-edomex/ ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/feria-alfenique-2024.html . **[solid]**, though the 1630 date is local tradition.

**5.6 Amaranth and chocolate skulls.**
- Skulls made of **amaranth** ("alegría") or **chocolate** are common modern variants.
- The Mexica did make ritual figures of amaranth dough (*tzoalli*). But the claim that "Aztecs made amaranth skulls that became sugar skulls" (repeated by NPR's food blog) is a simplification without clear documentation.
- Chocolate skulls are modern.

*Sources:* https://nuevaescuelamexicana.sep.gob.mx/contenido/recurso/calaveras-de-azucar-amaranto-o-chocolate/ ; https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2012/11/01/163549325/day-of-the-dead-decoded-a-joyful-celebration-of-life-and-food ; https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_revista/pan-de-muerto-origen-historia-simbolismo/ . **[contested]** for the "Aztec amaranth skull" lineage. **[solid]** that amaranth figures existed.

**5.7 Mole and tamales.**
- Mexico's agriculture ministry calls **tamales** (Nahuatl *tamalli*) a food that "can't be missing" from any ofrenda. They usually come with atole.
- **Mole** (Nahuatl *mōlli*, "sauce"), usually with chicken or turkey, appears in almost every list.
- Both vary by region. (BK) Oaxaca, for example, is known for *mole negro*.
- Modern mole mixes native ingredients (chiles, cacao) with Old World spices.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/articulos/del-campo-a-la-ofrenda-dulces-y-platillos-para-dia-de-muertos ; https://www.personal.unam.mx/Docs/Cendi/dia-de-muertos-elementos-de-la-ofrenda.pdf ; https://www.npr.org/sections/thesalt/2014/10/29/359829045/decoding-the-food-and-drink-on-a-day-of-the-dead-altar . **[solid]**

**5.8 Atole, champurrado and chocolate.**
- **Atole** (Nahuatl *atōlli*) is a pre-Hispanic hot drink thickened with maize, often sweetened with piloncillo (raw cane sugar) and cinnamon.
- **Champurrado** is atole with cacao. Food historian José N. Iturriaga distinguishes it, made with water, from *atole de chocolate*, made with milk.
- Pre-Hispanic peoples made cacao drinks and atoles. Sugar and milk came after the Conquest.
- (BK) Hot chocolate with pan de muerto, or with *pan de yema* in Oaxaca, is a classic pairing.

*Sources:* https://www.elfinanciero.com.mx/food-and-drink/2022/01/21/es-prehispanico-asi-nacio-el-champurrado/ ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/articulos/del-campo-a-la-ofrenda-dulces-y-platillos-para-dia-de-muertos . **[solid]**

**5.9 Calabaza en tacha.**
- Squash or pumpkin candied in piloncillo with cinnamon (and sometimes guava, orange or clove).
- Its name comes from the ***tachos* or *tachas***, copper cauldrons used in colonial sugar mills (*trapiches*), where it was cooked in leftover syrup.
- It is a typical sweet on ofrendas in central Mexico.
- When the recipe began is debated: one claim points to 18th-century Puebla convents.

*Sources:* https://mexiconewsdaily.com/lifestyle/food/taste-of-mexico-calabaza-en-tacha/ ; https://grupoanimal.mx/gastronomia/recetas/historia-receta-calabaza-en-tacha ; https://www.gob.mx/profeco/documentos/celebracion-del-dia-de-muertos-76082?state=published . **[solid]** for the name. **[contested]** for the date.

**5.10 Fruit.**
- Seasonal fruit, including:
  - **tejocotes** (Mexican hawthorn; (BK) Nahuatl *texōcotl*). In some communities these are offered especially to the "little dead", the children.
  - guavas, jícama, bananas,
  - and Old World fruits that arrived after colonization: **oranges, mandarins, limes, apples and sugarcane** (Profeco).
- Sugarcane stalks are also used to build arches.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/profeco/documentos/celebracion-del-dia-de-muertos-76082?state=published ; https://nuevaescuelamexicana.sep.gob.mx/contenido/coleccion/los-sabores-de-la-ofrenda/ ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura/articulos/del-campo-a-la-ofrenda-dulces-y-platillos-para-dia-de-muertos . **[solid]**

**5.11 Mucbipollo, or pib (Yucatán Peninsula).**
- The signature dish of **Hanal Pixán**: a large tamal of maize dough and lard, stuffed with chicken and pork in a tomato and chile sauce.
- It is wrapped in banana leaves and **cooked underground in a *pib* (earth oven)** on hot stones.
- Explanations of the name differ. The most common is Maya *muk-bil*, "buried", plus Spanish *pollo*, "chicken". Other names are *pibipollo* and *pib*.

*Sources:* https://laroussecocina.mx/blog/el-pib-o-mucbipollo-el-esperado-sabor-peninsular-del-dia-de-muertos/ ; https://www.elfinanciero.com.mx/food-and-drink/2021/11/01/mucbipollo-el-platillo-que-sale-de-la-tierra-para-recibir-a-los-difuntos/ ; https://merida.anahuac.mx/noticias/que-es-el-pibipollo ; https://www.poresto.com/turismo/gastronomia/2024/9/26/mucbipollo-o-pibipollo-las-variantes-del-platillo-tradicional-del-hanal-pixan.html . **[solid]** for the dish. **[contested]** for the etymology.

## 6. Symbols and art

**6.1 Calavera and calaca.**
- ***Calavera*** means "skull". It also names sugar skulls, the satirical verses (6.5) and the skeleton prints.
- ***Calaca*** is colloquial Mexican for a skeleton figure.
- Playful, lively skeletons dancing, cooking or playing music are a core part of the holiday's art.

*Sources:* https://www.britannica.com/topic/calaveras ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/smithsonian-latino-center/2018/10/23/literary-calaveras/ . **[solid]**

**6.2 La Catrina, part 1: Posada's "Calavera Garbancera".**
- **José Guadalupe Posada** was a printmaker. He was born in Aguascalientes on Feb 2, **1852**. (Britannica gives 1851; the Met, the Clark Art Institute and most other sources give 1852.) He died in Mexico City on Jan 20, **1913**.
- Around **1910–1912** he made an etching of a skeleton woman wearing nothing but an extravagant French-style plumed hat.
- It mocked ***garbanceras***. The word comes from chickpea (*garbanzo*) sellers and was slang for people, often of Indigenous descent, who pretended to be European and rich and denied their roots. It was satire about social pretension and class in the Porfirio Díaz era.
- The first known publication is a **1913** Day of the Dead broadside from publisher Antonio **Vanegas Arroyo**, printed after Posada's death. Its title begins: "Remate de calaveras alegres y sandungueras…"
- According to curator Rubén C. Córdova, **the texts on the four Catrina broadsides were not written by Posada**.

*Sources:* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Calavera_Catrina ; https://glasstire.com/2019/11/02/jose-guadalupe-posada-and-diego-rivera-fashion-catrina-from-sellout-to-national-icon-and-back-again/ ; https://britannica.com/biography/Jose-Guadalupe-Posada ; https://www.clarkart.edu/microsites/posada/about-the-exhibition/posadas-calaveras ; https://historia.nationalgeographic.com.es/a/historia-catrina-caricatura-belica-simbolo-dia-muertos_20418 ; https://www.jornada.com.mx/2013/01/20/opinion/032a1cap . **[solid]**, with small date differences between sources.

**6.3 La Catrina, part 2: Diego Rivera.**
- In his mural ***Sueño de una tarde dominical en la Alameda Central*** ("Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in the Alameda Central"), Rivera painted the skeleton full-length at the center:
  - in an elegant dress, with a **feathered boa often read as Quetzalcóatl, the feathered serpent**;
  - arm-in-arm with Posada,
  - holding the hand of Rivera himself as a boy,
  - with Frida Kahlo behind them.
- Most sources date the mural to **1946–47**. Some say 1947, and a few 1947–48.
- From then on the figure was known as **"La Catrina"**, from *catrín*, "dandy". Rivera made the name famous.
- The mural was painted for the Hotel del Prado. After the 1985 earthquake damaged the hotel, the mural was moved to the **Museo Mural Diego Rivera** in Mexico City.

*Sources:* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sue%C3%B1o_de_una_Tarde_Dominical_en_la_Alameda_Central ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/sueno-de-una-tarde-dominical-en-la-alameda-diego-rivera.html ; https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/diego-rivera-mural-museum/?lang=en ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Catrina ; https://unamglobal.unam.mx/sabes-como-nacio-la-catrina/ . **[solid]**. The year varies (1946–47 or 1947), and the Quetzalcóatl reading of the boa is an interpretation.

**6.4 "La muerte es democrática…"**
- This saying is very widely attributed to Posada, roughly: "Death is democratic: in the end, rich or poor, everyone ends up a skull."
- No source names the print, newspaper or year it comes from. Posada mostly engraved images, and other writers wrote the verses.
- Recommendation: say "a saying often attributed to Posada", or use it as a theme without quoting it.

*Sources:* https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/2017/11/01/1198493 ; https://artesdemexico.com/blog/las-calaveras-de-jos%C3%A9-guadalupe-posada ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Calavera_Catrina . **[contested or myth: the attribution is unverified]**

**6.5 Calaveras literarias (calaveritas).**
- Short **rhyming, funny poems that "kill off" living people**: friends, family, teachers, politicians and celebrities. They imagine Death (often La Catrina or a *calaca*) coming for the person and tease their habits.
- They are written among friends and families, printed in newspapers, read in schools and broadcast. The spirit is affectionate fun.
- **Origin:** they came from 19th-century satirical newspapers. Sources disagree on the first date:
  - **1849** or **1879**, both credited to *El Socialista* of Guadalajara;
  - an earlier antecedent, Fray Joaquín Bolaños's *La portentosa vida de la Muerte* (1792).
- Late-19th and early-20th-century broadsides by Vanegas Arroyo, illustrated by Posada, made them hugely popular.
- Game idea: kid-safe *calaveritas* about characters fit the tradition well.

*Sources:* https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/smithsonian-latino-center/2018/10/23/literary-calaveras/ ; https://www.gob.mx/agricultura%7Cdgsiap/es/articulos/calaveras-literarias-versos-que-se-escriben-en-visperas-del-dia-de-muertos ; https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/nacion/sociedad/dia-de-muertos-cual-es-el-origen-de-las-calaveritas-literarias/ ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/calaveritas-literarias-rima-y-picardia.html ; https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/top-ten-day-of-dead-mexico . **[solid]** for what they are. **[contested]** for the first date.

**6.6 Papel picado: origin and Huixcolotla.**
- Banners of **tissue paper (*papel de China*)** cut in stacks with chisels and punches on a lead plate.
- **San Salvador Huixcolotla, Puebla** is the best-known center. A **Sept 22, 1998** Puebla state decree declared the town's handmade papel picado **state cultural heritage**.
- When the craft began there is uncertain. Sources say:
  - "more than 150 years" ago,
  - the 1930s,
  - tissue paper reached Mexican haciendas in the mid-19th century,
  - claims of pre-Hispanic roots in *amate* bark paper (these are loose).
- Papel picado is used at many fiestas, not only Día de Muertos.
- Machine-made imitations threaten the handmade craft.

*Sources:* https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/papel-picado-huixcolotla.html ; https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/papel-picado-patrimonio-cultural-puebla.html ; https://www.ladobe.com.mx/2021/10/san-salvador-huixcolotla-el-corazon-del-papel-picado/ ; https://www.milenio.com/cultura/pueblo-de-papel-picado_2 ; https://www.horniman.ac.uk/story/papel-picado-mexicos-festive-paper-art/ . **[solid]** for Huixcolotla and the 1998 decree. **[contested]** for the older origins.

**6.7 Papel picado color meanings.**
- Color "meaning" lists circulate widely: purple for mourning or Catholicism; pink for joy; white for purity or children; black for the land of the dead; yellow or orange for the sun or marigolds; and so on.
- **The lists contradict each other.** For example, yellow means "light" in one list and "the elderly" in another, and pink means "joy" in one and "the nation" in another. One article says artisans "have attributed" meanings, which suggests a recent convention.
- **No ethnographic or academic source documents a fixed traditional code.**
- The safest explanation is that papel picado as a whole represents wind or air and festivity.

*Sources:* https://www.informador.mx/cultura/Dia-de-Muertos-Este-es-el-significado-de-los-colores-del-papel-picado-20231025-0082.html ; https://elporvenir.mx/cultural/dia-de-muertos-el-significado-de-los-colores-del-papel-picado/916938 ; https://www.eluniverso.com/larevista/sociedad/que-significa-el-papel-picado-de-dia-de-muertos-nota/ . **[contested or myth: there is no fixed traditional color code; the meanings are modern and inconsistent]**

**6.8 Alebrijes.**
- Invented by **Pedro Linares López** (1906–1992), a *cartonero* (papier-mâché artisan) from Mexico City.
  - In **1936**, during a high fever, he dreamed of strange creatures while voices shouted the made-up word "¡alebrijes!" Details of the story vary.
  - British filmmaker Judith Bronowski's **1975** documentary about him helped spread the craft, partly through workshops.
  - Oaxacan carver **Manuel Jiménez** of San Antonio Arrazola adapted it to **copal wood** carving. The carved version spread to **San Martín Tilcajete**, La Unión Tejalapan and other towns.
  - Linares won Mexico's **Premio Nacional de Ciencias y Artes** (folk arts category) in **1990**.
- **Alebrijes are not traditionally part of Día de Muertos.**
  - The Museo de Arte Popular's **Desfile de Alebrijes Monumentales** (first held Oct 20, 2007, as "Noche de los Alebrijes") is held in October, close to the holiday.
  - Pixar's ***Coco* (2017)** made them famous as **spirit guides**, which is a film idea.

*Sources:* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedro_Linares ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alebrije ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Jim%C3%A9nez_Ram%C3%ADrez ; https://lammuseum.wfu.edu/2020/10/mexico-create-a-colorful-alebrije/ ; https://www.mdzol.com/bbc-mundo/2021/6/29/pedro-linares-lopez-el-artista-mexicano-padre-de-los-alebrijes-al-que-google-le-dedica-su-doodle-169252.html/amp (BBC Mundo) ; https://afana.org/bronowski.htm ; https://www.milenio.com/cultura/alebrijes-desfilaran-en-la-ciudad-de-mexico ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico_City_Alebrije_Parade . **[solid]** (1936, Linares, Oaxaca adaptation, 2007 parade). **[contested or myth: "ancient spirit guides" is not true; the idea comes from *Coco*]**

**6.9 Monarch butterflies.**
- Millions of monarchs migrate more than 4,000 km from Canada and the US to the oyamel fir forests of the **Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve** in Michoacán and the State of Mexico. They arrive in **late October to early November**.
- Mexican government sources record the belief that **they are, or carry, the souls of the dead**:
  - **Purépecha**: the monarch was "the soul of the dead", and its arrival announced the visit of the dead on Nov 1–2 (SEMARNAT, CONANP).
  - **Mazahua**: souls travel inside or on the butterflies. INPI's Mazahua chronicle is titled "Xepje: El alma de los muertos".
  - **Matlatzinca** (INPI).
- INAH adds that the Mexica, Teotihuacanos, Mixtecs, Otomí and others wove the monarchs into their worldview.
- Popular customs include not shooing away a butterfly that visits on Nov 1–2.
- (BK) The reserve has been a UNESCO World Heritage natural site since 2008.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/semarnat/articulos/las-monarcas-en-el-dia-de-muertos ; https://www.gob.mx/inpi/es/articulos/cronicas-mazahuas-xepje-el-alma-de-los-muertos ; https://www.gob.mx/cms/uploads/attachment/file/589719/antologia-dia-de-muertos-pueblos-indigenas-de-mexico-inpi.pdf ; https://www.inah.gob.mx/foto-del-dia/mariposa-monarca-simbolo-ancestral ; https://www.gob.mx/conanp/articulos/dia-de-muertos-180962 . **[solid]** as a belief of specific communities. **[varies]** as a general Mexican belief.

**6.10 The Xoloitzcuintle.**
- Mexico's ancient hairless dog, also called the Xolo.
- Its name is Nahuatl *xōlōitzcuintli*, from **Xólotl** plus ***itzcuintli*, "dog"**.
- **Xólotl** was a dog-headed god, the twin or companion of Quetzalcóatl. He was linked to the evening star, death and transformation, and guided the sun through the underworld. In a creation myth he goes with Quetzalcóatl to Mictlán to recover bones to make new humans. Versions vary.
- Dogs were buried with the dead as guides (INAH). The Codex Laud shows a dead person with a Xolo before Mictlantecuhtli.
- INAH archaeozoologist Raúl Valadez links dogs and death partly to their scavenging habits.
- The Xolo is now a national symbol, partly thanks to Dante in *Coco* (see 2.5).

*Sources:* https://www.inah.gob.mx//foto-del-dia/xoloitzcuintle-companero-en-el-viaje-al-mictlan ; https://arqueologiamexicana.mx/mexico-antiguo/simbolismo-del-perro ; https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/llega-a-colima-la-exitosa-exposicion-temporal-xolos-companeros-de-viaje ; https://mna.inah.gob.mx/deviaje.php?pl=Xolos_Companeros_de_viaje_1 ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Xolotl-Aztec-god ; https://unamglobal.unam.mx/global_tv/xoloitzcuintle-el-perro-mexicano-asociado-a-la-muerte/ . **[solid]** for the core. **[varies]** for myth details.

**6.11 The "Legend of the cempasúchil" (Xóchitl and Huitzilin).**
- In this romantic legend, the girl Xóchitl becomes the flower and her love Huitzilin, the hummingbird, returns to her.
- Mexican government and INAH outreach pages present it as a "leyenda".
- Its age could not be determined in this research, and no early colonial source for it was found. It may be a modern retelling.
- Present it as "a popular legend", not as an Aztec myth.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/siap/articulos/el-dia-de-muertos-y-la-leyenda-de-la-flor-de-cempasuchil ; https://inah.gob.mx/images/interactivos/20221101_interactivo_rompcabezas_leyenda-cempasuchil/Cempasuchil/inicio.html . **[contested: origin unverified]**

**6.12 Catrina costumes and face paint.**
- Dressing as Catrinas and painting faces as skulls is a popular modern way to join festivals and parades: the Mexico City parade (since 2016), the Aguascalientes festival, and events in the US.
- It is not an old ritual of the holiday (BK).
- Game note: it is fine as festival fun, but don't present it as the ritual core.

## 7. Cemetery traditions

**7.1 Cleaning and decorating graves.** Families weed, clean and repaint graves and decorate them with cempasúchil, candles and often a small ofrenda of food. UNESCO describes families placing petals, candles and offerings along the path from home to the cemetery, and placing favorite dishes on both the home altar and the tomb.
*Sources:* https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/00054 ; https://americanindian.si.edu/online-resources/topic-listing?topic=3 . **[solid]**

**7.2 Vigils, music and food at the cemetery.** In many communities families keep an **all-night vigil (*velación*)** at the grave. They pray, eat, remember, and sometimes bring musicians, from mariachi and brass bands to local *sones*. Examples:
- Janitzio (Michoacán),
- Mixquic (Mexico City),
- Santa Cruz Xoxocotlán (Oaxaca),
- the muerteadas of Oaxaca's Etla valley.

*Sources:* see 7.3, 7.4 and 8.2. **[varies by region/family]**

**7.3 "La Alumbrada" in San Andrés Mixquic (Tláhuac, Mexico City).**
- On the **night of Nov 2**, from about 7 p.m., the town switches off artificial lights. The cemetery around the **16th-century church of San Andrés Apóstol** glows with thousands of candles placed on the graves.
- The 2026 activities run Oct 29 – Nov 2.
- Mixquic's cemetery sits right by the church.
- (A claim that Mixquic itself is UNESCO-listed is inaccurate; see 1.7.)

*Sources:* https://www.cronica.com.mx/metropoli/2026/10/28/dia-de-muertos-en-mixquic-2026-como-llegar-actividades-fechas-y-horarios/ ; https://www.infobae.com/mexico/2024/11/01/por-que-es-importante-el-dia-de-muertos-en-san-andres-mixquic/ ; https://www.maspormas.com/ciudad/enterate/asi-se-celebra-el-dia-de-muertos-en-mixquic/ . **[solid]**

**7.4 Janitzio and Lake Pátzcuaro (Michoacán): *Animecha Kejtzitakua*.**
- The Purépecha ceremony for the souls. The name is translated by Michoacán's state culture ministry as **"ofrenda para las ánimas"** ("offering for the souls").
- Spellings vary: *Animecha Kejtzitakua*, *ánimecha k'ejtsitakua*, *Ánimeecheri Kéjtsïtakua*.
- Families row to the island cemetery of **Janitzio**, historically in canoes with candles, and keep vigil with cempasúchil, candles and the dead person's favorite food. The main vigil is the **night of Nov 1–2**.
- ***Kejtzitakua Zapicheri*** is the part for children (*angelitos*).
- The ceremonies also take place at other lakeside Purépecha cemeteries: **Tzintzuntzan**, Jarácuaro, Tzurumútaro, Santa Fe de la Laguna and others.
- Purépecha altars use a wooden frame hung with bread, fruit and sugar figures.
- Heavy tourism has strained Janitzio. One 2026 report (single source) says the cemetery is closed to tourists this year for repairs, with island families keeping access.
- A popular legend, the love story of the Purépecha prince and princess Mintzita and Itzihuapa, is often told about Janitzio.

*Sources:* https://cultura.michoacan.gob.mx/noticias/secum-acercara-el-simbolismo-de-la-ofrenda-purepecha-al-museo-del-estado/ ; https://cultura.michoacan.gob.mx/boletines/la-fiesta-de-animas-purepecha-llego-a-los-pinos/ ; https://matadornetwork.com/es/dia-de-muertos-en-janitzio-michoacan/ ; https://cambiodemichoacan.com.mx/2021/11/01/noche-de-muertos-en-janitzio/ ; https://yucatanmagazine.com/day-of-the-dead-in-janitzio-no-tourists-allowed/ . **[solid]**. Spellings **[vary]**. The 2026 closure is unconfirmed.

**7.5 "¿Me da mi calaverita?" and the *campanero*.**
- From about Oct 31 to Nov 2, children go door to door, to shops and around schools asking **"¿Me da mi calaverita?"** ("Will you give me my little skull?"). They receive candy, fruit, bread or coins.
- In the past, kids carried lanterns made from hollowed *chilacayote* squash or boxes with a candle inside.
- Its origin is not documented. The usual explanation is 18th-century poor children asking for food for their own ofrendas. A "pre-Hispanic boy" story also circulates, and El Financiero calls it far from reality.
- It now mixes with Halloween costumes.
- In **Mixquic** the custom is the ***campanero***: kids ring a bell, pray at the ofrendas and receive bread, fruit or tamales.

*Sources:* https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/me-da-mi-calaverita-la-frase-que-ha-acompanado-a-la-ninez-en-dia-de-muertos.html ; https://www.infobae.com/mexico/2025/11/01/me-da-mi-calaverita-el-origen-de-la-frase-mas-dulce-del-dia-de-muertos-en-mexico/ ; https://www.elfinanciero.com.mx/nacional/2022/10/25/dia-de-muertos-2022-cuando-y-porque-se-pide-calaverita-en-mexico/ ; https://www.gastrolabweb.com/tendencias/2026/10/22/de-donde-viene-la-tradicion-de-dar-calaverita-y-obsequiar-dulces-a-pequenos-en-dia-de-muertos-70592.html . **[solid]** for the practice. **[contested]** for its origin.

## 8. Regional celebrations in Mexico

**8.1 Michoacán (Purépecha region: Pátzcuaro, Janitzio, Tzintzuntzan and more).**
- *Animecha Kejtzitakua* vigils (see 7.4).
- Arches and altars hung with bread, fruit and sugar figures.
- The monarch butterfly belief (6.9).
- The "culto a las ánimas" of Tiríndaro, which INAH exhibited at the National Museum of Anthropology.

*Sources:* https://cultura.michoacan.gob.mx/noticias/secum-acercara-el-simbolismo-de-la-ofrenda-purepecha-al-museo-del-estado/ ; https://inah.gob.mx/boletines/el-culto-a-las-animas-de-tirindaro-michoacan-llega-al-museo-nacional-de-antropologia . **[solid]**

**8.2 Oaxaca.**
- **Muerteadas** (Etla valley, especially **San Agustín Etla**):
  - Costumed troupes (*comparsas*) parade with brass bands through the **night of Nov 1–2**, sometimes until midday Nov 2.
  - At each stop they act out a comic verse play about a drunk who dies and comes back to life.
  - Characters include devils in suits covered with hand-sewn bells (reportedly 30–50 kg), "widows", skeletons on stilts, doctors and more.
  - **Comparsas** also parade in Oaxaca City.
- **Tapetes de arena** (sand carpets):
  - Pictures made of colored sand, sawdust, lime, seeds and flowers on the floor. They are originally a **funeral custom** of the Central Valleys, made for the *novenario* (nine days of prayers) and the "levantada de cruz" ceremony, and kept alive notably in **Santa Cruz Xoxocotlán**.
  - Large decorative versions are now displayed for Día de Muertos.
  - Their imagery is mostly Catholic, for example symbols of the Passion of Christ.
- **Xandu'** (Isthmus Zapotec, e.g., **Juchitán**):
  - The word is usually explained as from Spanish *santo*, "saint" or All Saints.
  - The main days are **Oct 30–31**, with prayers starting days earlier.
  - Altars are set up in the home. The first celebration after a death is called *biyé'* or *xandu' yaa*.
- **Breads:** see 5.3.

*Sources:* https://oaxaca.eluniversal.com.mx/mas-de-oaxaca/por-que-las-muerteadas-de-etla-son-de-las-mas-representativas-de-oaxaca/ ; https://www.nvinoticias.com/cultura/agenda/en-la-fiesta-mas-viva-de-todas-muerteadas-y-comparsas-invadiran-oaxaca/178691 ; https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/tapetes-de-arena-tradicion-de-comunidades-indigenas-de-oaxaca/1549165 ; https://nytraditions.org/digital-heritage/oaxacan-sand-painting-dia-de-los-muertosday-dead ; https://archivo.eluniversal.com.mx/estados/2014/impreso/la-fiesta-de-los-muertos-96673.html ; https://www.istmopress.com.mx/artecultura/biye-o-xandu-en-juchitan-por-victor-teran/ . **[solid]**. The Xandu' etymology is **[contested]** (Spanish loanword or older Zapotec word).

**8.3 Yucatán: Hanal Pixán.**
- The name is Yucatec Maya, ***hanal* "food" + *pixán* "soul" = "food of the souls"**. It is also spelled *Janal Pixán*.
- Schedule:

| Date | Name | For whom |
|---|---|---|
| Oct 31 | *U Hanal Palal* | children |
| Nov 1 | *U Hanal Nucuch Uinicoob* | adults |
| Nov 2 | *U Hanal Pixanoob* | all souls ("misa pixán") |

- **Bix** or *ochavario*: offerings and candles again eight days later, a farewell. INAH linguist Fidencio Briceño Chel describes it.
- Small children wear **red or black thread bracelets** (often with nine knots) so that visiting souls don't "take a liking" to them or make them ill. Customs vary by town.
- The signature food is **mucbipollo/pib** (5.11).
- Mérida holds large public altar exhibitions.

*Sources:* https://www.merida.gob.mx/animas/tradiciones.php ; https://www.poresto.com/yucatan/2025/11/3/que-es-el-bix-tradicion-maya-despues-del-hanal-pixan-en-yucatan.html ; https://www.poresto.com/yucatan/2022/10/18/por-que-se-les-amarra-un-hilo-los-bebes-durante-el-hanal-pixan-en-yucatan.html ; https://www.poresto.com/yucatan/2023/10/26/hanal-pixan-familias-de-peto-se-protegen-con-pulseras-rojas-negras-ante-la-llegada-de-los-finados.html . **[solid]** for the meaning and dates. **[varies]** for the details.

**8.4 Pomuch, Campeche: cleaning the bones (*Choo Ba'ak*).**
- At least about three years after burial, remains are moved to small wooden boxes in ossuaries.
- Every year, roughly **Oct 26 – Nov 2**, families **gently clean the bones** with brushes and change the **embroidered white cloths**, described as the souls' "clothing".
- The practice mixes Maya and Catholic belief.
- Campeche declared it **state intangible heritage in Oct 2017**. Workshops now teach it to children because of concerns that young people are losing it.
- Game note: it is respectful and loving in context, but needs careful, age-appropriate framing.

*Sources:* https://www.jornada.com.mx/noticia/2025/11/01/estados/inculcan-a-las-nuevas-generaciones-la-tradicional-limpieza-de-huesos-en-pomuch-campeche ; https://www.unotv.com/estados/campeche/en-pomuch-limpian-los-huesos-de-sus-difuntos-para-recibirlos-en-el-dia-de-muertos/ ; https://www.tvazteca.com/aztecanoticias/limpieza-huesos-en-pomuch-patrimonio-ancestral-dia-muertos . **[solid]**

**8.5 The Huasteca: Xantolo.**
- The Huasteca spans parts of Hidalgo, San Luis Potosí, Veracruz, Tamaulipas, Querétaro and Puebla. Nahua and Teenek communities take part.
- **The name** is usually explained as coming from Latin ***Sanctorum***, as in *Festum Omnium Sanctorum*, the feast of All Saints. In Nahuatl speech it became something like *Xantolo(n)*.
  - An alternative: Spanish *santo* + *todos* adapted into Nahuatl.
  - One specialist says the proper Nahuatl name is *Mijkailuitl*, "feast of the dead".
- **Dates:** the core is about **Oct 30/31 – Nov 2**, followed by *octavas* and *veintenas*. Some communities start much earlier: in Acatitla, Chicontepec, preparations begin with the first sowing on Jun 24, and in San Luis Potosí the official start is Sep 29 (San Miguel).
- **Big arches** of palm, cempasúchil and mano de león (4.15).
- **Huehues** ("old ones"): masked dance troupes who go from house to house.

*Sources:* https://www.milenio.com/cultura/xantolo-la-fiesta-huasteca-de-todos-los-santos ; https://www.gob.mx/inpi/articulos/xantolo-en-la-huasteca-hidalguense ; https://www.inah.gob.mx/boletines/nahuas-de-chicontepec-veracruz-colocan-altar-de-muertos-y-recrean-el-xantolo-en-el-museo-nacional-de-antropologia ; https://www.uaeh.edu.mx/noticias/7440/ ; https://revistas.inah.gob.mx/index.php/ocarinah/en/article/view/21907 ; https://revistaetnobiologia.mx/index.php/etno/article/view/118 . **[solid]**. The etymology is the most-cited explanation but **[contested]**.

**8.6 Mexico City.**
- **Mixquic:** the Alumbrada (7.3).
- **Gran Desfile de Día de Muertos:**
  - First held **Oct 29, 2016**.
  - Widely reported as inspired by the fictional parade in the opening of the **James Bond film *Spectre* (2015)**, which the city then "made real".
  - One outlet (Grupo Animal) credits citizen artists' groups and calls the timing a coincidence.
  - Either way, it is **a modern event, not an ancient tradition**.
- **Desfile de Alebrijes Monumentales:** run by the Museo de Arte Popular since **Oct 20, 2007**.
- **Ofrenda monumental in the Zócalo:** held every year, with themes such as the Revolution in 2024. It was decentralized to six sites in 2025. The year it started was not found.
- **UNAM Megaofrenda:** a university festival **since 1997**, which started at the Faculty of Medicine and has changed venues.

*Sources:* https://www.eluniversal.com.mx/tendencias/como-surgio-el-desfile-de-dia-de-muertos-en-cdmx-conoce-el-origen-de-esta-tradicion/ ; https://cnnespanol.cnn.com/2026/10/27/viajes/video/dia-muertos-desfile-james-bond-mexico ; https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/noticias/2016/10/30/desfile-de-muertos-en-ciudad-de-m-xico-inspirado-en-pel-cula-del-agente-007--spectre- ; https://grupoanimal.mx/estilo-de-vida/desfile-dia-muertos-cdmx-origen ; https://www.milenio.com/cultura/alebrijes-desfilaran-en-la-ciudad-de-mexico ; https://www.timeoutmexico.mx/ciudad-de-mexico/que-hacer/ofrenda-monumental-zocalo ; https://www.chilango.com/que-hacer/cdmx-tendra-6-ofrendas-monumentales-de-dia-de-muertos-donde-estaran/ ; https://www.chilango.com/ocio/megaofrenda-de-la-unam-diego-rivera/ . **[solid]**. The *Spectre* inspiration is widely reported, with one dissenting account.

**8.7 Aguascalientes: Festival de Calaveras.**
- The city is **Posada's birthplace** and home of the Museo José Guadalupe Posada.
- The festival grew from a craft market of *calaveras* near the old Los Ángeles and La Cruz cemeteries in the **early 1990s** (1991–94; sources differ). 2024 was billed as its 30th anniversary.
- It has a large **Desfile de Calaveras** and drew about 375,000 visitors in 2024.

*Sources:* https://theguadalajarareporter.net/index.php/news/news/regional/58919-aguascalientes-is-nation-s-day-of-the-dead-capital ; https://www.unotv.com/estados/aguascalientes/mas-de-375-mil-visitantes-en-festival-de-calaveras/ ; https://www.razon.com.mx/estados/2022/10/20/festival-cultural-de-calaveras-llegara-a-todos-los-municipios-de-aguascalientes-tere-jimenez/ . **[solid]**. The start year **[varies]**.

**8.8 Puebla.**
- **Huaquechula's monumental ofrendas**, in the Atlixco valley:
  - Altars **3–5 m tall**, tiered and covered in white, shiny cloth, built by families **for someone who died in the past year**.
  - At the center: the photo, with a mirror in front.
  - Families open their homes to visitors. Schedule: **Oct 28** for accident victims, **Oct 31** for children, **Nov 1–2** for adults.
  - They can cost families tens of thousands of pesos.
  - The ofrendas are **state cultural heritage of Puebla** (one source says since 1997). They are **not** individually UNESCO-listed.
- **Huixcolotla's papel picado** (6.6).

*Sources:* https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/huaquechula-la-magnificencia-de-la-muerte.html ; https://www.excelsior.com.mx/nacional/huaquechula-el-municipio-de-las-ofrendas-monumentales/1275803 ; https://www.eluniversalpuebla.com.mx/que-hacer/dia-de-muertos-puebla-altares-monumentales-de-huaquechula-una-tradicion-muy-poblana/ ; https://www.poblanerias.com/2019/11/ofrendas-de-huaquechula-patrimonio-cultural-de-puebla/ . **[solid]**

**8.9 State of Mexico.**
- **Toluca's Feria del Alfeñique** (5.5).
- **Mazahua and Matlatzinca** monarch beliefs (6.9).
- The **Metepec** clay-mold tradition (5.4).
- INPI documents distinct customs among the state's Indigenous peoples.

*Sources:* https://www.gob.mx/inpi/es/articulos/fiestas-de-muertos-en-los-pueblos-indigenas-del-estado-de-mexico . **[solid]**

**8.10 Morelos: Ocotepec.**
- Families who lost someone that year make **"ofrendas nuevas"**. (BK) Some are life-size figures of the deceased laid out with clothes, a sugar-skull head and offerings.
- Visitors bring candles, and the family shares food with them. Four candles in a cross bless the soul's paths.

*Sources:* https://www.mexicodesconocido.com.mx/dia-de-muertos-en-ocotepec-cuernavaca.html . **[varies]**. The life-size figure detail is (BK).

**8.11 Indigenous diversity overall.** INPI's anthology and its "11 altares indígenas" feature show how much practice differs among Mexico's Indigenous peoples (Otomí, Yaqui, Nahua, Maya, Mazahua and many others). They are good sources for authentic regional detail.

## 9. Around the world

**9.1 Guatemala.**
- **Nov 1** (*Día de Todos los Santos*): families visit cemeteries and decorate graves.
- **Barriletes gigantes** (giant kites, many meters across) are flown or displayed in **Sumpango** and **Santiago Sacatepéquez**, Kaqchikel Maya towns. In Santiago they fly in the cemetery. They are said to carry messages to the dead or to ward off bad spirits.
- **Fiambre**: a huge cold salad with dozens of ingredients (cold cuts, sausages, cheeses, pickled vegetables, beets), eaten on Nov 1.

**9.2 Bolivia.**
- **Todos Santos (Nov 1–2):** families set up tables of offerings for the souls. They include ***t'antawawas*** (bread "babies": *t'anta* "bread" + *wawa* "baby", Aymara/Quechua), bread ladders and horses to help the souls travel, sugarcane, flowers and favorite foods.
- A widespread Andean belief is that the souls arrive at noon on Nov 1 and leave on Nov 2.
- **Día de las Ñatitas** (about **Nov 8**, a week after; centered on La Paz's General Cemetery): people bring decorated **human skulls** (*ñatitas*, "little snub-noses"), considered protectors, with flowers, hats, coca leaves and candles. The Catholic Church doesn't officially endorse it.
- Game note: this is sensitive (real human remains).

**9.3 Ecuador.**

**9.4 Peru.**

**9.5 Philippines.**

**9.6 Brazil.** **Dia de Finados (Nov 2)** is a national holiday with visits to cemeteries, flowers, candles and Mass. It is generally more solemn. **(BK)** **[solid (BK)]**

**9.7 European parallels: background for "how much is European".**
- **Spain:** *Todos los Santos* cemetery visits; bone-shaped marzipan sweets called *huesos de santo*.
- **Italy and Sicily:** the *Festa dei Morti* (Nov 2), when, by tradition, the dead "bring" children gifts; **sugar figures (*pupi di zucchero*)**; marzipan fruit; and *ossa dei morti* ("bones of the dead") cookies.
- These show that bone-shaped sweets and sugar figures for All Souls existed in Catholic Europe.

**9.8 United States.**
- Public celebrations grew in the **1970s with the Chicano movement**. Artists reclaimed Mexican and Indigenous heritage, and community celebrations followed:
  - **Self Help Graphics & Art** (East Los Angeles) held early celebrations, usually dated **1972 or 1973**.
  - **Galería de la Raza** (San Francisco's Mission District) held early Day of the Dead exhibitions from about **1972**. The Mission District procession dates from the early 1980s.
  - **The National Museum of Mexican Art** (Chicago) has held a large annual exhibition, reportedly **since 1987**.
  - **Hollywood Forever Cemetery** (Los Angeles) has held a big festival since about **2000**.
  - Cities with large Mexican-American communities, such as San Antonio, hold large public events.
- Smithsonian museums (NMAI, the Smithsonian Latino Center) run annual programs. In 2026 there are free family events on Oct 31 in Washington, D.C. and New York.
- Scholarly reference: Regina Marchi, *Day of the Dead in the USA* (Rutgers University Press, 2009).
- Further reading found by search, not reviewed: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/day-of-the-dead-la ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/blogs/smithsonian-american-art-museum/2020/10/30/traditions-and-change-transformation-dia-de-los-muertos-united-states/ ; https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/altered-altars-changing-traditions-dia-de-los-muertos ; NMMA: https://artsandculture.google.com/story/nuestras-historias-day-of-the-dead-national-museum-of-mexican-art/mQWRb7ZeQriSIA?hl=en ; Smithsonian events: https://latino.si.edu/about/press-room/smithsonian-celebrates-day-dead
- **(BK)** for the founding dates. **[solid]** for the Smithsonian program.

**9.9 Pixar's *Coco* (2017).**
- It premiered at the Morelia International Film Festival and opened in Mexico in late Oct 2017 and in the US on Nov 22, 2017.
- It won two Academy Awards: Best Animated Feature and Best Original Song ("Remember Me").
- It greatly boosted the holiday's worldwide visibility.
- **Things *Coco* invented, not traditional beliefs:**
  - the Land of the Dead as a city,
  - the "Final Death" when no one remembers you,
  - needing your photo on an ofrenda to cross a border,
  - the marigold-petal bridge,
  - alebrijes as spirit guides.
- **A cautionary tale for game makers:** in **2013 Disney filed to trademark "Día de los Muertos"** and withdrew it after public backlash. Pixar then hired Mexican and Mexican-American cultural consultants, including cartoonist Lalo Alcaraz, who had drawn a protest cartoon.

- Spanish **"v" sounds like a soft "b"**. Single "r" is a quick tap, and **"rr" is rolled**.

## 11. Sensitivity notes for the game team

- Treat Día de Muertos as a **living family and religious tradition**, not a "spooky theme". The dead are welcomed loved ones.
- **Name the peoples** whose traditions you show (Purépecha, Yucatec Maya, Nahua, Teenek, Zapotec, Mazahua and others), and show regional variety instead of one "standard" version.
- Show **religious items** (crosses, saints, the Virgin of Guadalupe) and Indigenous ritual objects (copal, *sahumador*) respectfully. Avoid making them collectible "loot" or puzzle props that get broken or misused.
- Don't make **Mictlantecuhtli and Mictecacíhuatl villains or devils**. Don't merge **Santa Muerte** into the holiday (BK).
- Rituals with **real human remains** (Pomuch bone cleaning; Bolivia's *ñatitas*) are respectful in context but need careful, age-appropriate framing, or should be left out.
- Adult ofrendas often include **alcohol and cigarettes**. You can show "favorite drinks" without featuring them, but don't claim they never appear.
- Label **modern additions** honestly: the CDMX parade (2016), the alebrije parade (2007), the pet day (about 2019), *Coco*-style spirit guides, papel picado color meanings and Catrina face paint.
- Avoid **inventing "ancient Aztec legends"**. When a story's age is uncertain (the cempasúchil legend; the exact nine levels of Mictlán), say "a popular legend" or "one version".
- Lessons from *Coco*: hire **Mexican and Mexican-American cultural consultants** to review the script and art, and don't try to trademark the holiday's name (BK).

## 12. Claims that could NOT be verified (or that remain disputed)

- UNESCO's own page could not be opened. The 2003 and 2008 years and the element name are confirmed through search results and INAH. The UNESCO text's mention of the **maize cycle** is (BK).

- **Italian missionaries** bringing sugar skulls: no scholarly source found.
- The **pan de muerto "sacrifice/heart bread"** origin story: undocumented legend.
- **Papel picado color meanings**: no traditional basis documented.
- **The first calaveras literarias**: 1849 vs 1879 (*El Socialista*, Guadalajara) is unresolved.
- **Toluca's 1630 alfeñique date**: local tradition with only partial documentation.
- **Oct 27 pet day origin** (said to be a 2019 proposal by a pet funeral company): reported by several outlets, but not independently confirmed.

- **The Mexico City parade's origin**: *Spectre* inspiration (widely reported) vs a citizen-group origin (one source).
- **The "popoxcomitl" term**: modern, weakly attested.
- **The age of the Xóchitl and Huitzilin cempasúchil legend**: unknown.
- **The start year of the Zócalo ofrenda monumental**: not found.
- **The 2026 Janitzio cemetery closure to tourists**: single source.
- **The Huaquechula state-heritage year (1997)**: single source.
- **The Aguascalientes festival's first year**: early 1990s; the exact year varies.
